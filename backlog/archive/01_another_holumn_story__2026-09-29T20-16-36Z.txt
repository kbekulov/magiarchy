## There Is No Eleventh Floor

I knew the building had ten floors because I had worked there for six years.

That fact became important later.

At the time, it was just another useless certainty in a life built almost entirely out of useless certainties.

The elevator arrived at 22:49.

I stepped inside.

Pressed **1**.

The doors closed.

**9**

**8**

**7**

I checked my phone.

My sister had written:

*You alive?*

I replied:

*Leaving now.*

**6**

**5**

**4**

The elevator gave the same little vibration it always gave between floors.

**3**

**2**

**1**

I reached for the doors.

They did not open.

The elevator continued downward.

**0**

I frowned.

There was one parking level under the building.

Fine.

**-1**

Then:

**-2**

I looked up.

There was no minus two.

I pressed **1** again.

Nothing.

**-3**

Door Open.

Nothing.

Alarm.

Nothing.

**-5**

I took out my phone.

No signal.

**-8**

I remember saying, “Come on.”

To the elevator, apparently.

Then something scraped across the roof.

I stopped moving.

The scraping stopped too.

For perhaps ten seconds, neither of us did anything.

Then something heavy settled above me.

The ceiling panel bowed inward.

One dent appeared.

Then four more beside it.

Fingers.

Pressing downward.

The display changed.

**-14**

The elevator stopped.

The doors opened.

My office was outside.

Same grey carpet.

Same ceiling tiles.

Same terrible beige walls.

Same framed evacuation plan opposite the elevator.

Except my office was on Floor Six.

I stayed inside.

The doors remained open.

The fingers above me pressed harder.

I ran.

The moment I crossed the threshold, the elevator shut behind me.

I turned.

There was no elevator.

Only wall.

Solid plaster.

I screamed for several minutes.

Nobody answered.

After that, I began looking for the stairwell.

At first the office was almost convincing.

Meeting Room C.

Kitchen.

Reception.

Meeting Room C again.

I stopped.

Turned around.

Walked back.

Meeting Room C.

Again.

Every desk had the same mug.

Every computer displayed the same blank black screen.

The wall clock said **02:17**.

My phone said **22:53**.

I found the stairwell.

Concrete stairs.

Green handrail.

Normal.

I started climbing.

“One.”

Landing.

“Two.”

Landing.

“Three.”

The next door said:

**FLOOR 22**

“No.”

I went back down.

One flight.

**23**

Down again.

**24**

I stopped trusting the signs.

Started counting steps.

Sixteen.

Landing.

Sixteen.

Landing.

Sixteen.

The next door opened onto a residential hallway.

Dark green wallpaper.

A child’s bicycle.

A row of apartment doors.

The building had never contained apartments.

I closed it.

Three knocks came from the other side.

Then a little girl said:

“Wrong floor.”

I ran.

That became the first rule I learned.

**Do not answer voices behind doors.**

I learned the second rule later.

**Do not follow crying.**

The crying came from a bathroom.

A woman was inside one of the stalls.

She said she was lost too.

I almost cried from relief.

Then I saw her feet beneath the door.

Her heels faced me.

Her toes faced the toilet.

I backed away.

The crying stopped.

The stall opened.

I ran before I saw the rest.

I wrote things on my forearm after that.

**DO NOT FOLLOW CRYING**

**RED DOORS LIE**

**CHECK CEILING FIRST**

**DO NOT TRUST FLOOR NUMBERS**

At some point I added:

**WOMAN IN WHITE IS NOT HUMAN**

I do not remember meeting the woman in white.

That frightened me almost as much as the sentence.

My phone eventually changed to 02:17.

Then never changed again.

Battery: 73%.

Always 73%.

Hours passed.

Or minutes.

Or neither.

I found a parking garage fifty floors below ground with windows showing daylight.

I found an office where every employee photograph showed empty chairs.

I found a hallway that became narrower the farther I walked until my shoulders scraped both walls.

I found one door with my apartment number on it.

I did not open it.

Behind it, my mother called my name.

My mother had been dead nine years.

I sat outside that door for a very long time.

Then I heard something inside say:

“You know I’m not her.”

I ran again.

Eventually I reached a stairwell.

The landing read:

**47**

I sat down and started laughing.

Floor Forty-Seven.

Ten-floor building.

Of course.

Then I heard footsteps above.

Normal ones.

One step.

Another.

No dragging.

No clicking.

No bare feet.

That made them worse.

A black boot appeared around the staircase.

Then another.

Dark trousers.

A long blue coat.

A young woman came down from Floor 48.

Black hair.

Blue eyes.

Maybe twenty-something.

She stopped when she saw me.

Her eyes went first to my hands.

Then my shoes.

Then the writing on my arm.

Then the stairwell behind me.

“You’re alive.”

Not hello.

Not who are you.

*You’re alive.*

I stood too quickly and nearly fell.

She caught my shoulder.

“Easy.”

“Who are you?”

“Can you walk?”

“Who are you?”

“Can you walk?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

She looked at the landing number.

Then touched the wall beside it.

Not the number.

The concrete.

She knocked twice.

Listened.

Knocked again.

Then looked upward.

I said, “Forty-eight isn’t real.”

“I know.”

“You came from there.”

“Yes.”

“You know this place?”

“No.”

That answer terrified me.

She crouched near the edge of the landing and ran two fingers through the dust.

There were footprints.

Mine.

Dozens of them.

Going both directions.

I stared.

“I haven’t been here before.”

She glanced at me.

“Probably.”

“What does that mean?”

“Don't worry about it yet.”

She stood and removed a small metal object from her coat. I thought it was a weapon.

It was a coin.

She dropped it down the stairs.

We listened.

Clink.

Clink.

Clink.

Then, several seconds later:

Clink.

From above us.

I looked upward.

She did too.

Her expression tightened.

“What?”

“We don’t go down.”

“Why?”

“The coin did.”

“That doesn't make sense.”

“No.”

She looked almost pleased by that.

Not happy.

Focused.

As though something had finally confirmed a suspicion.

She started climbing upward.

I grabbed her sleeve.

“Up?”

“Yes.”

“I’ve been going down for hours.”

“That’s probably why you’re on forty-seven.”

I stared at her.

She stopped.

Looked at my hand gripping her coat.

Then at me.

“You don't have to understand it.”

“I would like to.”

“So would I.”

Something moved underneath the stairs below us.

She immediately put one finger against her lips.

We waited.

A hand slowly curled around the underside of the flight beneath.

Then another.

Long grey fingers.

The woman did not attack.

She watched.

The hands crawled sideways beneath the concrete.

Never crossing the painted yellow safety stripe along the landing edge.

Her eyes narrowed.

She stepped backward.

The hands followed.

Stopped at the stripe.

She stepped forward.

They retreated.

I whispered, “What are you doing?”

“Learning.”

The thing beneath the stairs began making a wet clicking sound.

She picked up a loose metal bolt from the floor and tossed it across the yellow stripe.

The hands exploded toward it.

She nodded once.

“Stay inside the paint.”

“What?”

“Inside the yellow lines.”

“Why?”

“Because it hasn't crossed them.”

“That’s it?”

“That’s enough.”

We climbed.

The thing followed us beneath the stairs.

Never across the marked landing.

Floor 48.

Then 12.

Then 9.

Then 67.

She ignored every number.

I eventually asked:

“How do you know where we’re going?”

“I don’t.”

I nearly stopped.

She added:

“But the numbers are part of the trap.”

“How do you know?”

“Because they want you to react to them.”

She pointed at the stairwell itself.

“Concrete stays concrete. Handrail stays continuous. Gravity still works most of the time. Air is moving upward through the shaft. Trust things the place has to maintain. Not the things it wants you to read.”

That was the first moment I understood why she was still alive.

She was not stronger than the place.

She was better at refusing its arguments.

At another landing, she stopped before opening the door.

The number said **6**.

I almost laughed.

“That’s my floor.”

She looked at me.

Then at the handle.

“Which way does your stairwell door open?”

I stared.

“What?”

“Normally.”

“Out.”

“This opens in.”

She stepped away.

The door handle turned by itself.

Something on the other side whispered:

“You found it.”

The black-haired woman drew a pistol.

That was somehow reassuring.

A gun I understood.

She fired twice through the door.

Something screamed.

Not died.

Screamed.

She grabbed my wrist.

“Run.”

We went up.

Not down.

I did not question it anymore.

Later we crossed an office corridor where every door was numbered **1004**.

She made me walk backward through half of it.

“Why?”

“Because the corridor length changes when you look at the end.”

“How did you notice that?”

“You stopped hearing your own footsteps echo.”

I had not noticed.

She had.

At another junction, we found three identical staircases.

She closed her eyes.

Listened.

Chose the middle.

“What did you hear?”

“Ventilation.”

“They all sound the same.”

“No.”

To me, they absolutely did.

She made mistakes too.

Once she opened a door and immediately slammed it again.

Something hit the other side hard enough to bow the metal.

She looked genuinely frightened then.

“Wrong?”

“Very.”

“What was there?”

“Don’t know.”

“You looked.”

“Not long enough.”

We left.

That frightened me more than if she had explained it.

Eventually the stairwell ended.

Not at a landing.

The concrete steps simply stopped in darkness.

No wall ahead.

No floor.

Just black space.

She crouched.

Threw another coin.

We never heard it land.

Something screamed several floors below.

Closer than before.

I whispered:

“What now?”

She looked at the gap.

Then behind us.

Then at me.

For the first time since I met her, she looked uncertain.

Her right hand flexed.

Something faintly blue flickered beneath the skin around her fingers.

She immediately clenched the hand shut.

The light vanished.

I saw it.

“What was that?”

“Nothing.”

“That was not nothing.”

“Forget it.”

The scream came again.

Closer.

She looked annoyed now.

Not at the creature.

At herself.

She removed her glove.

Her palm had old burn scars.

Some very old.

Some less old.

She looked at the gap again.

“No.”

I had no idea who she was talking to.

Then something began climbing the stairwell wall below us.

I saw its head first.

Sideways.

Then shoulders.

Then six arms unfolding around the concrete.

The black-haired woman drew the pistol and fired.

The first round tore through its face.

It kept coming.

Second.

Third.

It accelerated.

She shoved me behind her.

“Stay there.”

She fired until the slide locked back.

The thing climbed faster.

She swore.

Then blue fire appeared in her hand.

Not gradually.

Suddenly.

Brilliant enough that every shadow in the stairwell snapped backward.

I screamed.

She screamed too.

Not from fear.

Pain.

The flame crawled over her hand and halfway up her wrist.

Her sleeve began smoking.

For one instant she nearly lost control of it.

She clenched her teeth and drove the burning hand against the concrete wall rather than toward the creature.

Blue fire raced through the yellow paint along the landing.

A line.

A boundary.

The creature reached it.

The moment its first hand crossed, the limb vanished in blue light.

It shrieked.

The woman staggered backward, holding her wrist.

The flame had burned her too.

Not badly enough to stop her.

Badly enough that I understood.

This was not a weapon she casually possessed.

It was something she survived using.

The creature tried another route.

The blue line flared.

It retreated.

She grabbed me with her unburned hand.

“Move.”

“But there’s nowhere.”

“There will be.”

She kicked the emergency door beside us.

Nothing.

Again.

Nothing.

She looked at the frame.

Then at the wall.

Then back at the impossible darkness where the stairs should have continued.

“It's copying transitions.”

“What?”

“Every time we choose a route it gives us another route.”

The creature screamed behind us.

She looked at the closed emergency door.

Then said:

“So we stop choosing.”

I stared.

She turned around.

Put her back against the door.

And waited.

“What are you doing?”

“Nothing.”

“That thing is coming.”

“I know.”

“What are we waiting for?”

“For the floor to decide.”

I thought she had finally gone insane.

Then the elevator chimed.

Behind us.

Where there had been no elevator.

She smiled.

Barely.

“There.”

The creature slammed into the blue boundary.

The flame flickered.

She winced.

“Now.”

We ran into the elevator.

The display read:

**∞**

I nearly stayed outside.

She dragged me in.

The doors closed.

The creature reached the threshold.

One blue-burning hand hit the gap.

She kicked it away.

The doors shut.

The elevator moved.

I pressed myself against the wall.

She held her burned hand against her coat.

The skin was red and blistering.

“You could do that the whole time?”

She looked at me.

“No.”

“You just did.”

“I could also jump out a window.”

I stared.

“That doesn’t make it useful.”

The elevator chimed.

Doors opened.

Floor Six.

My office.

Normal fluorescent lights.

Normal carpet.

She did not step out.

She looked first at the ceiling.

Then the floor.

Then the evacuation plan.

Then the fire extinguisher.

Finally she threw another coin.

It landed normally.

She stepped out.

Only then did I follow.

The stairwell clock read 22:55.

Six minutes.

I started laughing.

She sat on the floor.

Not gracefully.

Just collapsed against the wall, breathing hard, inspecting her burned hand.

“You’re hurt.”

“Yes.”

“Because of me?”

She looked up.

“Don’t flatter yourself.”

That almost made me laugh normally.

Almost.

Then she took out her phone.

Signal.

She called someone.

“I have the witness.”

Pause.

“Alive.”

That word again.

My stomach tightened.

She listened.

“No. Don't send police.”

I looked at her.

She noticed.

Continued speaking.

“Yes. Church.”

Pause.

“Special envoys. Ordinary cover.”

Another pause.

“No integration. This is containment and re-education.”

I stepped backward.

She ended the call.

“What is that?”

She looked at me.

“What?”

“Re-education.”

Her face said she had hoped I had not heard.

“You really do listen when it becomes inconvenient.”

“Who did you call?”

“People who are going to clean this up.”

“Clean what up?”

She gestured vaguely around us.

“This.”

“There’s nothing here.”

“Exactly.”

I backed toward the corridor.

She remained where she was.

Did not reach for me.

That somehow made it worse.

“What are you?”

She sighed.

“That's becoming a complicated question.”

“Police?”

“No.”

“Military?”

“No.”

“Government?”

“Sometimes adjacent.”

I stared.

She rubbed her forehead.

Then looked at me as if making a decision she disliked.

“Are you religious?”

I thought I had misheard.

“What?”

“Religious.”

“Why?”

“Just answer.”

“No.”

She considered.

“Any denomination?”

“No.”

“Family?”

“My mother went to church.”

“What kind?”

“Catholic.”

She nodded.

“Good enough.”

“Good enough for what?”

She stood carefully.

Her burned hand trembled once.

She hid it inside the coat pocket.

“I’m afraid you’re going to have to surrender yourself to the Church for a few weeks.”

I stared.

“What?”

“Temporarily.”

“The Church.”

“Yes.”

“Which church?”

“Technically, yours will be easiest.”

“I just told you I'm not religious.”

“They won’t mind.”

I laughed.

“You’re kidnapping me.”

She winced.

“That is an unnecessarily hostile description.”

“What would you call it?”

“Protective doctrinal containment.”

“That sounds worse.”

“Yes.”

“Are you insane?”

“Possibly, but not about this.”

I started walking away.

She did not stop me.

At the end of the corridor, the elevator stood open.

I froze.

She waited.

I looked at the stairwell.

Then the elevator.

Then at her.

She raised one eyebrow.

“That’s approximately the problem.”

I stayed.

“What happens at the Church?”

“You sleep.”

“What else?”

“You eat.”

I stared.

She thought for a second.

“Three meals a day.”

“That is your sales pitch?”

“The beds are decent.”

“You’re joking.”

“No.”

That was somehow much funnier.

I laughed once.

She continued:

“They’ll ask questions. A lot of questions. They’ll explain certain things to you in a controlled way. Then they’ll explain why you cannot repeat them.”

“And if I refuse?”

Her expression changed.

Not threatening.

Sad.

That was worse.

“I strongly recommend that you don't make this complicated.”

“Will they hurt me?”

“I don't expect them to.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No.”

“What will they do to my head?”

She went quiet.

I felt cold.

“What will they do?”

“They'll make sure you can return to ordinary life without endangering the boundary.”

“That sounds like you'll brainwash me.”

She looked away.

“That's a vulgar description.”

“Is it wrong?”

A long pause.

“Not completely.”

I backed away again.

“No.”

“I understand.”

“No, you don't.”

“I probably understand better than you think.”

“You rescued me just to hand me over.”

“Yes.”

The honesty stunned me.

She continued before I could speak.

“Because the alternative is letting you go home with knowledge you are not allowed to possess.”

“Not allowed?”

“Yes.”

“According to who?”

“The Church.”

“I don't belong to the Church.”

“That has never mattered as much to the Church as people imagine.”

I stared.

She looked exhausted now.

Twenty years older than when she had walked down Floor Forty-Eight.

“They are not going to recruit you.”

“I should be grateful?”

“They are not going to make you clergy.”

“Wonderful.”

“They are not going to kill you.”

I stopped.

She realized immediately that she should not have said that.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You just said they're not going to kill me.”

“I was listing positives.”

“That was on the list?”

She closed her eyes.

“Forget I said that.”

“I cannot forget anything about tonight.”

Her eyes opened again.

“That is precisely the issue.”

Footsteps approached from the far end of the corridor.

Three people.

Two men.

One woman.

Dark civilian clothes.

One of the men wore a clerical collar.

Nothing about him looked like a priest.

The way he scanned doors looked military.

The woman carried a medical case.

The second man looked at the walls, the ceiling, every camera.

Special envoys.

I did not know that term yet.

The black-haired woman did.

One of them looked at her burned hand.

“You used it?”

“Once.”

His expression hardened.

“Necessary?”

“Yes.”

He accepted that immediately.

No admiration.

No amazement.

Only calculation.

That scared me.

The woman with the medical case approached me.

I moved back.

The black-haired woman stepped between us.

“Slowly.”

The medic stopped.

For one second, I understood something else.

They listened to her.

Not completely.

But enough.

I looked at the black-haired woman.

“Lynleit.”

She looked at me.

It was the first time I had used her name since the stairwell.

“Will I remember you?”

Nobody spoke.

Lynleit looked toward the Church people.

Then back at me.

“I don't know.”

That was the most frightening answer of the night.

I looked at her burned hand.

At the blue coat.

At the corridor.

At the elevator I would never use again.

Then at the people waiting to take me somewhere.

“Will I remember Floor Forty-Seven?”

She thought about it.

“Probably.”

“Will I believe myself?”

Another pause.

“Probably less.”

That should have sounded terrible.

Instead, part of me felt relief.

She noticed.

Of course she did.

The medic offered me her hand.

I did not take it.

I stood by myself.

Lynleit gave me a tired little nod.

“That’s better.”

I walked toward the strangers from the Church.

After three steps, I turned.

“Why did you come for me?”

Lynleit looked genuinely confused by the question.

“You were trapped.”

“That’s it?”

“Yes.”

“You nearly burned your hand off.”

She glanced at it.

“It’ll heal.”

“You could have died.”

She shrugged.

“Possibly.”

I stared at her.

She seemed suddenly irritated by the conversation.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“Then go.”

I turned away.

Behind me, I heard one envoy ask:

“What did you establish?”

Lynleit answered:

“Floor numbers are bait.”

“What else?”

“Vertical direction is unreliable. Structural continuity is less unreliable.”

“And the entities?”

“Some respect boundaries they inherit from the original architecture.”

“Blue flame effective?”

A pause.

“Too expensive.”

That was the last thing I heard before the elevator doors closed.

Not the office elevator.

I refused that one.

They took me through the stairs.

Real stairs.

Six floors.

I counted every single landing.

For the next seventeen days, I lived in a monastery that officially did not admit having me.

The beds were, irritatingly, quite good.

There were three meals a day.

Lynleit had not lied about that.

The rest was harder to describe.

Priests who did not speak like priests.

Doctors who asked questions about doors.

Men who wanted me to repeat Floor Forty-Seven until it sounded less like a place and more like a stress response.

They never told me magic was not real.

They taught me increasingly sophisticated ways to explain why I should never use that word.

By the time I left, I understood perfectly what had happened to me.

I also understood why saying it aloud would make me sound insane.

Sometimes I wonder whether that was the point.

Sometimes I wonder whether the person who entered that monastery ever actually came back out.

But I returned to work.

Eventually.

I took the stairs.

Always.

Months later, I saw a woman in a long blue coat across a railway platform.

Black hair.

Same posture.

She was looking at her phone.

I stared.

She looked up.

Our eyes met.

For one terrible second I knew her.

Then the train passed between us.

When it was gone, so was she.

I stood there for a long time trying to remember why my heart was beating so hard.

And somewhere inside me, beneath all the explanations the Church had carefully given me, a number surfaced without context.

Forty-seven.
