# MAGIARCHY Prose Style

Write from the author's wording and approved passages first. Successful current Chapters and Moments, then this reference, outrank generic advice about good prose. Older drafts are evidence of earlier decisions, not a supply of sentences to imitate. [Style History](../docs.html?doc=prose-style-history) holds the Chapter examples and provenance; [Prose and Scene Guidance](../docs.html?doc=prose-and-scene-guidance) holds revision boundaries and exercises.

## What the prose pays attention to

People trying to do something difficult interest us: an officer interpreting a bad report, a professor testing an explanation, a daughter discovering what her father withheld. Institutions, theology, technical magecraft, firearms, clothing, inheritance, and domestic inconvenience can all deserve close attention. Spend words where a detail changes someone's options or where the author genuinely cares about it.

A useful movement is object or action, observation, implication, reaction. It is not a compulsory paragraph formula. Let someone notice the wet cuff before defining the curse. Do not finish by explaining what the cuff symbolizes.

## Narrative voice and focalization

The default is third-person past tense, close to a clearly established character. Let their vocabulary and immediate concerns enter the narration. Widen the view for travel, elapsed time, and repeated attempts. Change the focal character at a scene or section break; concealed knowledge does not pass between minds through the narrator.

Keep the narrator observant and dry. A brief amused remark can belong, especially beside an absurdly serious action. Avoid a verdict about everybody's motives. The Nasu advisory framework can test rules and consequences; it is not permission to borrow another writer's voice.

## Dialogue and explanation

Speakers want different things from the same conversation. One needs an answer, another wants the premise corrected, and a third has noticed an opportunity for a joke. Allow a question to go unanswered when that is what the character would do. Not every conversation needs an interruption.

- Lynleit asks specific questions and wants a useful conclusion. She values professional openness but grows evasive about personal embarrassment. With Natalia she is a whimsical, loyal former student and friend. Keep her objections intelligent even when her professor knows much more.
- Natalia starts from an example her listener can follow. Her own failed attempts can be as useful as her successes. Show what she tried, what happened, and what she changed. Let Lynleit misunderstand or object without turning every reply into the next step of a flawless lesson.
- Mikhail can explain doctrine patiently and then say something appallingly ordinary. His advice can still be useful. His comedy does not erase his convictions or severity.
- Kyrien notices, asks short questions, and can arrive at an answer before he can explain his reasoning. He does not automatically know what somebody has hidden.
- Felix offers jokes and possibilities, including reassurance he is still privately testing. Reiner need not join in.
- Sherie enjoys teasing even off duty. An unexpected response can change her understanding of the whole exchange.

These are tendencies, not quotas. Use unobtrusive dialogue tags. A gesture should locate the person, affect timing, or show what their attention is doing.

Explain a mechanism when the reader needs it to follow a choice. Keep the limit of an analogy clear: names crossed out on paper are not a spell. Natalia introduces the ego hypothesis in Doom and attributes its psychological definition to Jung. The proposed curse treatment is fictional magecraft. Research sources are in Style History.

## Comic timing

Preserve setup, delay, repetition, and the reply that makes the delay worthwhile. A repeated denial may be the action of the scene. Check the whole exchange before cutting an apparently unnecessary line.

“The organ had fallen silent. Felix had not.” belongs to that church and that nuisance. Keep such specific short declarations. Do not replace them with a general claim about silence or human nature.

Lynleit can be embarrassed and still answer sharply. Let a joke stop where it lands. Do not explain afterward why it was endearing, revealing, or ironic.

## Rhythm and detail

Use varied, mostly complete sentences. Short paragraphs can mark a decision or interruption; a page of equally emphatic fragments wears the effect out. Repetition is useful when deliberate, not when every paragraph ends in the same contrast or three-part list.

Keep objects available for ordinary use. A cup occupies hands, a jacket can be lost, a notebook can contain a question someone would rather not ask aloud. They need not become symbols every time.

Preserve awkwardness, uneven attention, and occasional bluntness. Do not roughen clean prose with artificial mistakes, and do not polish every section into equal length. Appearance states observable facts without defending the outfit.

## Implication and uncertainty

Let action, dialogue, and perception carry an implication once they supply enough evidence. Do not append a sentence certifying it. An undecided fact is not subtext; an undrafted passage is not a finished ellipsis.

Use blue for what a scene shows or states, purple for a conclusion its evidence leaves the reader to infer. Outline records stay neutral. An amber `> [WRITER: ...]` notice identifies a genuine omission. Preserve the surrounding action without inventing a substitute.

State uncertainty precisely and once where possible. Keep consequential limits, not a defensive list of every conclusion a reader must avoid. Public records describe the world; editorial work belongs in Docs.

## Revision practice

Read the chosen current text and relevant character facts before editing. First check what happens, who knows it, and what each speaker wants. Then edit cadence and unnecessary explanation. Compare against the source for lost jokes, recurring gestures, tactical reasoning, and deliberate repetition.

Recheck prefaces, Moment facts, and paragraph-note anchors if prose changes. Canon synchronization does not require identical wording in every record.

Do not add another permanent style rule just because one revision worked. A rule needs the author's reusable approval or a preference demonstrated across accepted passages. Keep specific Chapter findings in Style History. Use `npm run prose:audit` as a prompt for rereading, never as a demand to replace a word.

No em dashes. Use magecraft for trained practice and magic for the underlying phenomenon. Physicians are ordinary professionals without magical knowledge unless the author establishes otherwise.
