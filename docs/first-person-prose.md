# First Person Prose

This is the mandatory writing and editorial standard for first-person narrative passages in MAGIARCHY, based on the author's supplied short-story transcript, analysed on 1 October 2026. It applies to Chapters, Moments, embedded witness accounts and future first-person stories, not just frightening scenes. The [general Prose Style](../docs.html?doc=prose-style) still governs the archive and third-person prose.

The preferred voice sounds like a particular person trying to give an intelligible account of something they experienced. Its force comes from ordinary expectations, practical actions, incomplete understanding and what the speaker finds difficult to admit. It does not depend on elaborate imagery, constant surprise or a supernatural subject.

The author explicitly requested hard rules for generation and audits. The FP rules below are therefore required acceptance criteria, not optional suggestions. Their application depends on the passage: an aftermath rule does not force a conclusion onto an excerpt, and a dialogue rule does not require invented conversation. Record a justified not-applicable result when a condition is absent. Do not turn conditional requirements into a fixed plot formula, word quota or compulsory emotional reaction. Explicit author directions and established story facts remain controlling.

## What creates the recognizable voice

### Spoken recollection rather than a polished performance

The narrators make room for a listener. They explain why they were present, what they normally expected and why a detail would have mattered to them. They sometimes correct themselves or admit that their reaction sounds foolish. This produces an uneven but intelligible account, with the speaker's attention guiding its length.

Frequent first-person verbs put the speaker back into the event: noticing, looking, thinking, trying, asking and deciding. Removing every perceptual filter would erase part of this voice. Equally, mechanically beginning every sentence with a first-person pronoun would copy a surface habit rather than the effect. The important distinction is whether the sentence conveys the speaker's actual process of apprehension.

### Two moments of knowledge

Most accounts use past tense for the event and present tense for the act of remembering. The speaker knows how the event ended, but the remembered self must still encounter the evidence in sequence. Brief later comments can express embarrassment, continuing affection, disagreement or a changed judgment. They are not permission to supply unseen motives or the archive's complete explanation.

The winter account is especially useful here: the narrator reports contradictory evidence and describes why part of the experience was withheld. The secondhand pedestrian account adds another boundary, between what the friend reportedly saw and what the framing speaker imagines. A reliable rendering preserves these boundaries even when the speaker is certain, frightened or mistaken.

### A sequence of changing practical problems

An ordinary activity leads to a specific disturbance. The person forms a plausible explanation, looks more closely or takes an action, encounters a detail that no longer fits, and revises what they can do. This is the main source of forward motion. It is not merely a succession of increasingly frightening adjectives.

Some accounts spend considerable time establishing a job, possession, relationship or route. Their unusual event matters because the earlier situation has given it a scale. The family witness story could not produce the same aftermath without establishing the familiar relative; the riding story needs the novice's enthusiasm and the friend's greater confidence.

This structure transfers to other genres. In a negotiation, an ordinary expectation may fail because of an unexpected clause. In comedy, an explanation can make an inconvenience worse. In a reunion, a familiar gesture can undermine an anticipated reception. The voice remains the person's attempt to understand and respond, without adding a monster, secret or crisis where none belongs.

### Sentence and paragraph rhythm

The source favors everyday verbs, contractions, direct emotional statements and simple clause connections. A longer explanation is often followed by a short identification or consequence. The rhythm is accumulative: a person looks, waits, checks something, speaks, and then reacts. Blunt sentences leave room for the event to matter without dressing every observation as a revelation.

Repeated references sometimes sustain fixation or retry an explanation. A stated fear can recur because the next observation changes its basis. This useful repetition must be distinguished from transcript duplication and redundant intensifiers. Short blocks help listening, but the transcript's recording format cannot justify breaking every sentence into a separate dramatic paragraph.

### Social discomfort and incomplete agency

The speakers worry about being caught, being rude, being laughed at, disappointing someone or admitting they had no plan. These concerns make choices particular. They can coexist with expertise, affection, ordinary enjoyment and appropriate action. A professional may still hesitate; a frightened witness may still perform a practical task.

Their limits are located in the scene: distance prevents hearing, snow obstructs a view, an explanation seems more socially acceptable, a route constrains movement. These are stronger than making a narrator generically helpless. The source also lets companions behave differently, providing reassurance, bravado, evidence or social pressure without becoming interchangeable witnesses.

## Mandatory rules for first person

### FP01 Keep a particular speaker

Write as the named or otherwise established narrator, not as a neutral author changing third-person pronouns. Select vocabulary, comparisons, expertise, ordinary concerns and what feels embarrassing from this person's background and present task. First person does not make every character chatty, frightened or colloquial in the same way. Avoid importing the source speakers' slang, professions or worldview.

Audit: Can the reviewer identify whose attention and language shape the passage, beyond the use of I?

### FP02 Separate experience from later knowledge

Establish whether the passage is retrospective testimony, an immediate account or another author-requested mode. Default to past-tense recollection with selective present-day commentary when no different mode is requested. Keep the experiencing self within what they could perceive and know at that point. Mark genuinely later knowledge or changed judgment as later; do not leak hidden canon through a convenient retrospective sentence.

Audit: Is each important fact available to the experiencing narrator, or clearly attributed to a permitted later discovery or report?

### FP03 Give the event an ordinary context

Provide the relevant activity, expectation, relationship or inconvenience that makes the event intelligible. Establish only the baseline the reader needs; a continuation can rely on the preceding scene. Do not force an opening confession or lengthy biography. Select details because they explain attention and choices, not because every scene must begin quietly.

Audit: Does the reader understand what the narrator was trying to do and what counted as ordinary for them here?

### FP04 Follow perception into interpretation

At a consequential change, give the reader the available cue and the narrator's plausible reading before treating that reading as settled knowledge. Let a new observation alter the interpretation when appropriate. Preserve certainty about a sensation separately from uncertainty about its cause. A mistaken inference can be strongly voiced without becoming an authorial fact.

Audit: Can the reviewer distinguish what was perceived, what the narrator inferred and what remains unknown?

### FP05 Make choices intelligible without explaining everything

Give a concrete reason for a consequential decision when it would otherwise seem arbitrary: duty, curiosity, pride, affection, habit, embarrassment or a practical constraint. Let the reason appear as thought, behavior or context. Do not append a psychological certificate to every action, defend all bad judgment or make fear automatically erase competence.

Audit: Do the important choices follow this person's situation and limits rather than an author's need to move the plot?

### FP06 Keep space and physical access legible

Anchor what matters: the obstruction, distance, route, object, position or person being approached. Track changes that determine sight, hearing, reach, movement and available tools. Reveal an object in time for its use to be comprehensible. Do not add a room inventory or tactical diagram to a scene that needs neither.

Audit: Can the reader follow how the narrator perceives and physically attempts the important actions?

### FP07 Stay with the consequential interval

Dramatize the important encounter through observation, attempt, response and changed options. Compress routine travel, repetition or elapsed time when nothing important is lost. Do not summarize the main experience as several terrible things happening, and do not turn every second into slow motion. Include hesitation or waiting when that interval changes the experience.

Audit: Has any decisive experience been skipped by summary, or padded beyond what attention and consequence justify?

### FP08 Use an unforced spoken register

Prefer intelligible everyday syntax and concrete verbs. Allow contractions, modest qualifications, direct emotional words and natural connective language where they suit the narrator. Keep necessary professional terms and explain them as this person would. Do not replace an ordinary word with an elevated synonym merely to make the passage sound literary, or imitate transcription errors to make it sound human.

Audit: Would this particular person plausibly tell the account in these words, and can it be understood aloud without rereading?

### FP09 Let rhythm follow attention

Mix mostly complete sentences with occasional fragments or short identifications when they serve recognition, urgency or spoken emphasis. Let explanatory passages have room. Change paragraph when attention, action, speaker or inference shifts. Neither uniform shortness nor uniform polish is acceptable as a substitute for scene rhythm.

Audit: Do sentence and paragraph changes support the developing attention rather than a repeated formatting pattern?

### FP10 Keep repetition when its function changes

Retain repeated questions, observations or attempts when they express fixation, test an explanation, expose hesitation, sustain a joke or gain a changed meaning. Remove duplicates that only restate an unchanged verdict. First-person filtering and common words are permitted; do not edit them out by quota. Repeated certainty claims must have a local reason, not act as a standard credibility device.

Audit: Does each conspicuous recurrence do further work, or merely insist again that the event was strange or frightening?

### FP11 Make feeling personal and practical

Allow the narrator to name fear, confusion, pleasure or embarrassment plainly. Couple sustained feeling to what they notice, attempt, avoid, withhold or physically struggle to do. Bodily responses can affect balance, speech, grip or attention, but do not impose a heartbeat-and-goosebumps routine. Ordinary concerns may persist during an extraordinary event.

Audit: Does emotion belong to this person's experience and behavior rather than a reusable list of symptoms or abstract labels?

### FP12 Let dialogue alter the encounter

When dialogue is present, let it change information, confidence, action, social pressure or the relationship. Preserve different interpretations, uneven confidence, ignored questions and ordinary speech where supported. A recollected paraphrase may be less exact than a quoted line, but clarify speakers and distinguish speech from thought in the written version. Do not fabricate verbatim dialogue the source or canon cannot support.

Audit: Does the conversation do something for these people, and is it clear who spoke, thought or later reported each important line?

### FP13 Keep other minds outside the viewpoint

Show companions through what the narrator can observe and reasonably interpret. Qualify consequential claims about their feelings or intentions unless they said them or the narrator later learned them. In secondhand accounts, retain attribution and mark the framing speaker's guesses. Do not repeatedly add he said when the attribution is already clear, but do not silently convert hearsay into witnessed fact.

Audit: Has the passage granted access to another person's mind or experience without a defensible source?

### FP14 Earn changes in intensity

Let escalation, relief, embarrassment or affection grow from new evidence, an altered relationship or changed options. Contrast can work through a familiar person acting differently or an ordinary activity continuing after disruption. Do not inflate every beat with superlatives, ominous forecasts, decorative metaphors or an obligatory reversal. A calm first-person scene may remain calm.

Audit: What concrete change earns each major shift in intensity, and what merely announces an atmosphere?

### FP15 End at a particular consequence

When a scene or account reaches its end, land on a specific action, changed relationship, unresolved observation, continuing inconvenience or earned reflection. The source permits a brief direct statement of lasting feeling; preserve it when it adds something personal. Do not append a generic lesson about life, darkness or fate. An unfinished excerpt must remain unfinished rather than acquire an invented resolution.

Audit: Does the ending leave this narrator with a particular consequence, without adding unsupported answers or a portable dramatic slogan?

### FP16 Transfer technique without transferring source content

Use the sample's methods with the target story's own people, setting, facts and tone. Correct obvious transcription defects in new writing; never reproduce them as style. Do not import source incidents, folklore, dialogue, technical claims or a fixed horror structure. Keep author-approved wording and project constraints intact, including the distinction between magic and magecraft and the ban on em dashes.

Audit: Is the resemblance carried by narrative technique rather than borrowed story material, copied phrases or recording artifacts?

## Editorial distinctions that matter

The transcript contains a false start around line 173, an apparent pronoun slip at line 117, inconsistent ridge lettering in the climbing excerpt and a mistranscribed painting reference. It also lacks normal dialogue punctuation in places. These are not preferred prose characteristics. Preserve the source unchanged for evidence, but do not reproduce these defects.

Repeated assurances, generic statements of terror and occasional stock bodily reactions occur in the samples. Their presence does not make them compulsory. Preserve their useful functions, such as a speaker defending an unlikely observation or admitting vulnerability, while writing a specific sentence appropriate to the new narrator. An isolated familiar comparison is not a mandate for dense metaphor.

The source's apparently contradictory description of how one apparition disappears illustrates why an audit must distinguish reported perception from continuity. In new prose, determine whether the contrast belongs to the narrator's attempt to describe it or is simply an accidental inconsistency. Do not turn an editorial accident into a new supernatural mechanism.

This standard explicitly permits first-person admissions, immediate reasoning, selective retrospective comment and purposeful repetition even when a generic concision pass would remove them. The existing house preference against redundant explanation still applies. Keep a thought that supplies a decision or changes the interpretation; cut a second narrator's verdict that only certifies what the reader already understood.

The sample's colloquial accounts do not override an established character's formal, educated, reserved or eccentric manner. A precise professional term can belong in plainspoken narration. MBTI can assist a characterization review but should not appear as a substitute for the narrator's concrete thoughts.

## Reusable drafting brief

Before drafting, establish the narrator, the narrative mode and time of telling, what they know, their immediate purpose, the practical geography, the relevant relationships, and the facts or dialogue that must be preserved. Identify the consequential interval that deserves real-time attention. These are planning questions, not material to recite at the start of the story.

Write in the speaker's ordinary register. Give the relevant baseline, let perceptions prompt provisional interpretations and choices, and let consequences change the account. Keep the body and practical world available. Let supporting characters differ. Use brief retrospective remarks only where the chosen mode and reveal boundaries allow them. Reach an appropriate consequence without manufacturing an explanation.

In a mixed-perspective Chapter, apply these requirements only to its first-person narrative passages. A first-person line of dialogue inside third-person narration does not change the Chapter's narrative perspective. Do not convert existing third-person Chapters merely to use this reference.

## Mandatory audit procedure

Read the target passage, relevant canon and this standard before reviewing. Identify first-person narrative spans explicitly; pronouns inside dialogue are not sufficient evidence. Review every applicable FP rule and retain passage locations supporting the judgment. Use pass, revise or not applicable with a specific reason. These findings belong in the task's review notes, not in fictional prose or a narrator's closing explanation.

First check viewpoint and factual boundaries. Next inspect the sequence of perception, reasoning, choices and physical consequence. Then read aloud for voice, rhythm and speech attribution. Finally compare the revision against the source for lost personality, practical detail, humor, repeated attempts and intentional uncertainty. Similarity to the sample is not permission to overwrite approved canon or dialogue.

Any applicable rule marked revise must be addressed before accepting the first-person draft, unless the author explicitly chooses the exception. A list of passing technical checks is not an editorial sign-off. Do not claim the prose matches the preference solely because it has short paragraphs or first-person pronouns.

Run `npm run prose:audit -- --first-person story/example.md` for a selected Markdown file; supply more paths after the flag for more files. The command prints the current FP checklist and limited review signals with source locations. It cannot judge personality, fictional truth, emotional effect or style fidelity. Its warnings are advisory, while the human or agent review against this document is mandatory. The default archive audit remains available and does not silently certify first-person compliance.

The original corpus remains private and need not exist on a public deployment or another checkout for the documented rules and audit tool to work. Future samples may refine the analysis; change the versioned standard explicitly rather than quietly weakening a hard rule.
