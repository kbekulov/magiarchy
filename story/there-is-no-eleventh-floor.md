# There Is No Eleventh Floor

I knew the building had ten floors because I had worked there for six years.

That fact became important later.

At the time, it was just another useless certainty in a life built almost entirely out of useless certainties.

The elevator arrived at 22:49.

I stepped inside.

Pressed **1**.

The doors closed.

**9**

**8**

**7**

I checked my phone.

My sister had written:

*You alive?*

I replied:

*Leaving now.*

**6**

**5**

**4**

The elevator gave the same little vibration it always gave between floors.

**3**

**2**

**1**

I reached for the doors.

They did not open.

The elevator continued downward.

**0**

I frowned.

There was one parking level under the building.

Fine.

**-1**

Then:

**-2**

I looked up.

There was no minus two.

I pressed **1** again.

Nothing.

**-3**

Door Open.

Nothing.

Alarm.

Nothing.

**-5**

I took out my phone.

No signal.

**-8**

I remember saying, “Come on.”

To the elevator, apparently.

Then something scraped across the roof.

I stopped moving.

The scraping stopped too.

For perhaps ten seconds, neither of us did anything.

Then something heavy settled above me.

The ceiling panel bowed inward.

The light was fixed to that panel. It came down with it, slowly enough that I could see the strip of clean metal it had covered. Dust fell on my phone.

I moved into the corner by the buttons. There was nowhere else to move. My shoulder kept the alarm pressed in, and I waited for somebody to ask what the emergency was.

The button stayed warm against my shoulder.

One dent appeared.

Then four more beside it.

Fingers.

Pressing downward.

The display changed.

**-14**

The elevator stopped.

The doors opened.

My office was outside.

Same grey carpet.

Same ceiling tiles.

Same terrible beige walls.

Same framed evacuation plan opposite the elevator.

Except my office was on Floor Six.

I stayed inside.

The doors remained open.

The fingers above me pressed harder.

I ran.

The moment I crossed the threshold, the elevator shut behind me.

I turned.

There was no elevator.

Only wall.

Solid plaster.

I hit the plaster with both hands.

There should have been a seam down the middle. I worked my fingernails along the wall where the doors had met, finding grit, then a shallow scratch I had made myself. Behind me the lights hummed.

“Hello?”

I waited through a breath I could not quite take.

“There's somebody here. I'm stuck.”

My voice went farther than it should have. I heard the last word come back from the kitchen.

I shouted again, louder, until my throat hurt. Between calls I put my ear to the wall. I wanted the scrape back. I wanted something on the other side to prove there was still an elevator.

Nobody answered.

After that, I began looking for the stairwell.

At first the office was almost convincing.

Meeting Room C.

Kitchen.

Reception.

Meeting Room C again.

I stopped.

Turned around.

Walked back.

Meeting Room C.

Again.

I put my phone on the carpet outside it and walked to the kitchen without looking away from the phone. Twelve steps. The little black rectangle remained beside the door.

From the kitchen I could see another Meeting Room C ahead of me.

There was a phone outside that one too.

I went back for mine. I did not look at the other one while I picked it up.

Every desk had the same mug.

Every computer displayed the same blank black screen.

The wall clock said **02:17**.

My phone said **22:53**.

I found the stairwell.

Concrete stairs.

Green handrail.

Normal.

I started climbing.

“One.”

Landing.

“Two.”

Landing.

“Three.”

The next door said:

**FLOOR 22**

“No.”

I went back down.

One flight.

**23**

Down again.

**24**

I stopped trusting the signs.

Started counting steps.

Sixteen.

Landing.

Sixteen.

Landing.

Sixteen.

The next door opened onto a residential hallway.

Dark green wallpaper.

A child’s bicycle.

A row of apartment doors.

The building had never contained apartments.

I closed it.

Three knocks came from the other side.

Then a little girl said:

“Wrong floor.”

I ran.

That became the first rule I learned.

**Do not answer voices behind doors.**

I learned the second rule later.

**Do not follow crying.**

The crying came from a bathroom.

A woman was inside one of the stalls.

She said she was lost too.

I almost cried from relief.

Then I saw her feet beneath the door.

Her heels faced me.

Her toes faced the toilet.

That was ordinary enough. What I could not account for was her voice, which came from just above the latch, close enough to stir the loose strip of paper stuck there.

“Please,” she said.

I bent a little, wanting to see whether somebody else was in the stall. The shoes did not turn. Neither moved when the voice came lower.

“I can see you.”

I backed away.

The crying stopped.

The stall opened.

I ran before I saw the rest.

I wrote things on my forearm after that.

**DO NOT FOLLOW CRYING**

**RED DOORS LIE**

**CHECK CEILING FIRST**

**DO NOT TRUST FLOOR NUMBERS**

At some point I added:

**WOMAN IN WHITE IS NOT HUMAN**

I do not remember meeting the woman in white.

That frightened me almost as much as the sentence.

I kept checking my phone while I walked. At first I could still watch the minutes change. I used them to decide when I ought to have reached the next landing.

Then it changed to 02:17.

I watched it for what felt like a full minute. Counted sixty breaths, lost count and started again. When I checked the clock on the wall, its second hand was moving. I waited for it to complete a circuit.

It reached the top and clicked backward one mark.

My phone still said 02:17.

Battery: 73%.

Always 73%.

Hours passed.

Or minutes.

Or neither.

I found a parking garage fifty floors below ground with windows showing daylight.

For a moment I could smell warm cars. I went toward a window, keeping close to a row of painted bays. Beyond the glass there was a strip of sky and the tops of trees.

I pressed my palm against it. The sky stayed in the same place when I crouched. So did the trees. I stood on tiptoe and saw no more of either.

Somewhere behind the parked cars, an engine turned over without starting. Once. Again.

I backed toward the stairwell. The engine stopped as soon as I touched its handle.

I found an office where every employee photograph showed empty chairs.

I found a hallway that became narrower the farther I walked until my shoulders scraped both walls.

I found one door with my apartment number on it.

I did not open it.

Behind it, my mother called my name.

My mother had been dead nine years.

I sat outside that door with my thumb over the call button beside my sister's name. Still no signal.

“You've left your bag,” my mother said.

I looked down. My bag was between my feet.

I could remember her saying that. Not on any particular day. It was one of the things she had been alive to say.

I put the phone away because I could not bear seeing my sister's name while that voice waited for an answer.

Then I heard something inside say:

“You know I’m not her.”

I ran again.

Eventually I reached a stairwell.

The landing read:

**47**

I sat down and started laughing.

Floor Forty-Seven.

Ten-floor building.

Of course.

Then I heard footsteps above.

Normal ones.

One step.

Another.

No dragging.

No clicking.

No bare feet.

That made them worse.

A black boot appeared around the staircase.

Then another.

Black laces running up above the knees. Dark tights, and the edge of fitted black shorts where the coat opened as she descended.

A long blue coat.

A young woman came down from Floor 48.

Very long dark hair, disarranged over one shoulder. A pale blouse buttoned high at the neck, with a narrow black ribbon.

Maybe twenty-something.

She stopped when she saw me.

Her eyes went first to my hands.

Then my shoes.

Then the writing on my arm.

Then the stairwell behind me.

“You’re alive.”

Not hello.

Not who are you.

*You’re alive.*

I stood too quickly and nearly fell.

She caught my shoulder.

“Easy.”

“Who are you?”

“Can you walk?”

“Who are you?”

“Can you walk?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

She looked at the landing number.

Then touched the wall beside it.

Not the number.

The concrete.

She knocked twice.

Listened.

Knocked again.

Then looked upward.

I said, “Forty-eight isn’t real.”

“I know.”

“You came from there.”

“Yes.”

“You know this place?”

“No.”

That answer terrified me.

She crouched near the edge of the landing and ran two fingers through the dust.

There were footprints.

Mine.

Dozens of them.

Going both directions.

I stared.

“I haven’t been here before.”

She glanced at me.

“Probably.”

“What does that mean?”

“Don't worry about it yet.”

She stood and removed a small metal object from her coat. I thought it was a weapon.

It was a coin.

She dropped it down the stairs.

We listened.

Clink.

Clink.

Clink.

Then, several seconds later:

Clink.

From above us.

I looked upward.

She did too.

Her expression tightened.

“What?”

“We don’t go down.”

“Why?”

“The coin did.”

“That doesn't make sense.”

“No.”

She looked almost pleased by that.

Not happy.

Focused.

As though something had finally confirmed a suspicion.

She started climbing upward.

I grabbed her sleeve.

“Up?”

“Yes.”

“I’ve been going down for hours.”

“That’s probably why you’re on forty-seven.”

I stared at her.

She stopped.

Looked at my hand gripping her coat.

Then at me.

“You don't have to understand it.”

“I would like to.”

“So would I.”

Something moved underneath the stairs below us.

She immediately put one finger against her lips.

We waited.

A hand slowly curled around the underside of the flight beneath.

Then another.

Long grey fingers.

The woman did not attack.

She watched.

The hands crawled sideways beneath the concrete.

Never crossing the painted yellow safety stripe along the landing edge.

Her eyes narrowed.

She stepped backward.

The hands followed.

Stopped at the stripe.

She stepped forward.

They retreated.

I whispered, “What are you doing?”

“Learning.”

The thing beneath the stairs began making a wet clicking sound.

She picked up a loose metal bolt from the floor and tossed it across the yellow stripe.

The hands exploded toward it.

She nodded once.

“Stay inside the paint.”

“What?”

“Inside the yellow lines.”

“Why?”

“Because it hasn't crossed them.”

“That’s it?”

“That’s enough.”

We climbed.

The thing followed us beneath the stairs.

Never across the marked landing.

Floor 48.

Then 12.

Then 9.

Then 67.

She ignored every number.

I eventually asked:

“How do you know where we’re going?”

“I don’t.”

I nearly stopped.

She added:

“But the numbers are part of the trap.”

“How do you know?”

“Because they want you to react to them.”

She pointed at the stairwell itself.

“Concrete stays concrete. Handrail stays continuous. Gravity still works most of the time. Air is moving upward through the shaft. Trust things the place has to maintain. Not the things it wants you to read.”

That was the first moment I understood why she was still alive.

She was not stronger than the place.

She was better at refusing its arguments.

At another landing, she stopped before opening the door.

The number said **6**.

I almost laughed.

“That’s my floor.”

She looked at me.

Then at the handle.

“Which way does your stairwell door open?”

I stared.

“What?”

“Normally.”

“Out.”

“This opens in.”

She stepped away.

The door handle turned by itself.

Something on the other side whispered:

“You found it.”

The black-haired woman reached behind her waist and drew a pistol.

It was small. Silver caught the light along its short slide, above a dark grip almost hidden in her hand. I had been imagining a much larger weapon, if I had been imagining one at all.

That was somehow reassuring.

A gun I understood.

She fired twice through the door.

Something screamed.

Not died.

Screamed.

She grabbed my wrist.

“Run.”

We went up.

Not down.

I did not question it anymore.

Beyond the next stairwell door was an office corridor where every door was numbered **1004**.

She made me walk backward through half of it.

My heel caught the edge of the carpet. She caught my sleeve before I went down.

“Small steps.”

I kept my eyes on the door we had entered through. It ought to have been getting farther away. Each time I blinked it seemed to have come a little closer.

Behind me she counted our steps under her breath. I could feel her hand at my elbow, correcting me when I drifted. I wanted to turn around so badly that my neck began to ache from refusing to do it.

At thirty-one she said, “Stop.”

I stopped with one foot still lifted.

Her hand moved from my elbow to my shoulder and turned me a little to the right. A door handle touched my back. She reached past me and opened it.

“Why?”

“Because the corridor length changes when you look at the end.”

“How did you notice that?”

“You stopped hearing your own footsteps echo.”

I had not noticed.

She had.

The door opened onto three identical staircases.

She closed her eyes.

Listened.

Chose the middle.

“What did you hear?”

“Ventilation.”

“They all sound the same.”

“No.”

To me, they absolutely did.

She made mistakes too.

At the next landing she opened a door and immediately slammed it again.

Something hit the other side hard enough to bow the metal.

She looked genuinely frightened then.

“Wrong?”

“Very.”

“What was there?”

“Don’t know.”

“You looked.”

“Not long enough.”

We left.

That frightened me more than if she had explained it.

Eventually the stairwell ended.

Not at a landing.

The concrete steps simply stopped in darkness.

No wall ahead.

No floor.

Just black space.

She crouched.

Threw another coin.

We never heard it land.

Something screamed several floors below.

Closer than before.

I whispered:

“What now?”

She looked at the gap.

Then behind us.

Then at me.

For the first time since I met her, she looked uncertain.

Her right hand flexed.

Her coat moved aside. Two black leather gloves hung over her belt, tucked so close to her waist that I had not noticed them before.

She took them off the belt, pulled one on, then the other. Her thumb worked around each cuff, checking that no skin remained between glove and sleeve.

A blue point appeared against the leather of her forefinger.

She closed her hand. The light vanished.

I saw it.

“What was that?”

“Nothing.”

“That was not nothing.”

“Forget it.”

The scream came again.

Closer.

She looked annoyed now.

Not at the creature.

At herself.

“Your jacket. Off.”

I looked down at myself as though she had asked for something I did not own.

“Over your head. Tuck your hands in. All the skin you can cover.”

“Why?”

She held up the gloved hand.

“Because you haven't got these.”

My zip caught halfway down. I pulled until the little metal tab bent, then got one arm out and dragged the other sleeve over my watch. She watched the stairs while I struggled.

“Will that burn me?”

“If you're exposed.”

“What about you?”

She pressed the seam at her wrist once more.

“That's what the gloves are for. Hurry.”

She looked at the gap again.

“No.”

I had no idea who she was talking to.

Then something began climbing the stairwell wall below us.

I saw its head first.

Sideways.

Then shoulders.

Then six arms unfolding around the concrete.

The black-haired woman drew the pistol and fired.

The first round tore through its face.

It kept coming.

Second.

Third.

It accelerated.

She shoved me behind her.

“Stay there.”

She fired until the slide locked back.

The thing climbed faster.

She dropped the empty magazine. I heard it hit a step and slide. Her other hand was already coming back with a spare.

The new magazine went into the little silver pistol. She brought it up again before the empty one had stopped rattling.

For three shots I believed we would be all right.

Each struck something. I could see that. A shoulder jerked; a hand lost its grip. But there was always another hand. The damaged face kept rising between them.

I tried to pull the jacket over my head. A grey finger came around the rail beside me and caught a sleeve. The cloth tightened across my throat.

She fired past my shoulder. The finger opened. My jacket went with it, slipping between the steps before I could catch the collar.

I stood with my hands over my ears. My mouth was open. I could not hear whether I was making a sound.

She kept firing until the pistol stopped again.

The thing put a hand on our landing.

She looked at the empty gun, then at the hand.

“Oh, have it.”

She threw the pistol into its face. The little silver shape struck with an ugly, solid knock. The head went sideways; two of the arms flung out to catch its weight.

She was already taking off her coat.

It came over my head before I understood what she was doing. Warm lining hit my mouth. She pushed me down beside the wall and pulled the coat across my knees.

“Hands inside. Don't move.”

“I can't see.”

“Good.”

I caught the lining in my teeth when I tried to answer. It smelled of cloth warmed by a person, with a faint trace of perfume. My right wrist was still outside. I drew it in.

The hem followed it.

Not by much. Just enough to settle under my hand where a strip of skin had been showing. I tried to remember whether she had tucked it there.

Her boots moved away from me.

Then blue fire appeared.

I saw it through the small opening beside my cheek. Not a glow slowly gathering. One instant there was grey concrete, the next there was so much blue that I could see every pit in it.

Her gloved hand struck the wall.

Fire ran down to the yellow stripe, raced along it and stopped at the broken end of the landing.

Then it turned upward.

I waited for it to reach the ceiling. It went past where the ceiling should have stopped it. Above us another yellow edge lit up, and another above that. Through the broken stairwell I could see blue landings hanging at wrong angles, close enough to touch one another, each with the same chip in the paint beside her hand.

There was no heat against my face. Outside the opening, the metal railing made a thin, tightening sound.

The thing reached through the nearest line.

Its fingers appeared in the flame above us.

Its wrist was still below.

For a moment it held both positions. Then the fingers opened, emptied of whatever had joined them to the hand, and fell upward out of sight.

I tried to crawl farther under the coat.

Something drew the opening shut against my cheek.

I did not feel a hand. I felt the weight of the cloth settle, slowly and firmly, while the woman made a short, strained sound several feet away.

“Stay down.”

The voice was hers. I held on to that.

Something struck the wall on the other side of my covered head. I felt the impact through my teeth. A little dust fell into the lining. I waited for the next blow, counting my own fingers inside the coat because they were the only things I could check.

One. Two. Three. Four.

The thumb was folded under my palm. I had to straighten it to convince myself it was there.

The creature shrieked.

Claws scraped from the stairs onto the wall beside me. I pressed both hands over my covered head. The blows climbed past it, stopped, then scattered downward in a hurry.

Blue light pulsed through the stitching.

The scraping receded down the wall. I waited for it to climb again.

The coat lifted from my face. She was crouching in front of me, both gloves still on. Blood ran from one nostril to her upper lip. She wiped it with the back of her wrist, looked at the smear, and pressed her lips together.

The blue edges above us flickered. She put her hand back on the wall until they steadied.

Then she caught my arm.

“Move.”

“But there’s nowhere.”

“There will be.”

She kicked the emergency door beside us.

Nothing.

Again.

Nothing.

She looked at the frame.

Then at the wall.

Then back at the impossible darkness where the stairs should have continued.

“It's copying transitions.”

“What?”

“Every time we choose a route it gives us another route.”

The creature screamed behind us.

She looked at the closed emergency door.

Then said:

“So we stop choosing.”

I stared.

She turned around.

Put her back against the door.

And waited.

I still had her coat around my shoulders. The hem reached the floor again, ordinary cloth in an ordinary heap. I kept my fingers away from its edge.

“What are you doing?”

“Nothing.”

“That thing is coming.”

“I know.”

“What are we waiting for?”

“For the floor to decide.”

I thought she had finally gone insane.

Something scraped along the stripe behind us. I started to turn.

“Here,” she said.

I looked at her instead. Blood had reached the corner of her mouth. Her hand shook against the wall, and I could hear her trying to make her breathing quiet.

The door handle pressed into my back. I wanted to try it. It was unbearable to stand against a door and not try it.

“How long?”

She did not answer.

The handle went down by itself.

She caught my wrist before I could reach for it. We stayed where we were.

Then the elevator chimed.

Behind us.

Where there had been no elevator.

She smiled.

Barely.

“There.”

The creature slammed into the blue boundary.

The flame flickered.

She winced.

“Now.”

We ran into the elevator.

The display read:

**∞**

I nearly stayed outside.

She dragged me in.

The doors closed.

The creature reached the threshold.

One blue-burning hand hit the gap.

She kicked it away.

The doors shut.

The elevator moved.

I pressed myself against the wall.

She pushed the coat down from my head but left it around me. Her hand rested on its shoulder seam until the shaking eased.

Then she peeled one glove back far enough to look underneath.

Unbroken skin. No blister. She checked the cuff before pulling it on again.

There was blood on her lip, though, and fresh blood at her nose. She pressed a folded tissue against it and leaned her head forward.

“You could do that the whole time?”

She looked at me.

“No.”

“You just did.”

“I could also jump out a window.”

I stared.

“That doesn’t make it useful.”

The elevator chimed.

Doors opened.

Floor Six.

My office.

Normal fluorescent lights.

Normal carpet.

She did not step out.

She looked first at the ceiling.

Then the floor.

Then the evacuation plan.

Then the fire extinguisher.

Finally she threw another coin.

It landed normally.

She stepped out.

Only then did I follow.

The stairwell clock read 22:55.

Six minutes.

I started laughing.

She sat on the floor.

Not gracefully.

Just collapsed against the wall, breathing hard. She took the gloves off finger by finger and draped them over her belt again. For a while she could not make the little fastening catch.

I tried to give the coat back. She shook her head without looking up.

“Keep it on a minute.”

“You’re hurt.”

“Yes.”

“Because of me?”

She looked up.

“Don’t flatter yourself.”

That almost made me laugh normally.

Almost.

Then she took out her phone.

Signal.

She called someone.

“I'm finished here. Your turn.”

Pause.

“Yes. Both of us.”

She sounded as though she had been kept waiting for a lift home. I could see the tissue reddening between her fingers.

She listened.

“No, no medical.”

She looked me over, from my face to the shoes I had been staring at for hours.

“He seems all right.”

I looked at her.

“Relatively,” she added.

She listened, then covered the phone.

“Are you religious?”

I thought I had misheard.

“What?”

“Religious.”

“Why?”

“Just answer.”

“No.”

“Family?”

“My mother went to church.”

“What kind?”

“Catholic.”

She uncovered the phone.

“Catholic, apparently. Though he doesn't seem particular.”

Pause.

“I was going to suggest the Orthodox. The food's better.”

She held the phone away from her ear for a moment.

“Well, you asked.”

I stepped backward.

She ended the call.

“Whose turn?”

She looked at me.

“What?”

“You said it was somebody else's turn.”

She folded the tissue over to find a clean part.

“Who did you call?”

“People who are going to clean this up.”

“Clean what up?”

She gestured vaguely around us.

“This.”

“There’s nothing here.”

“Exactly.”

I backed toward the corridor.

She remained where she was.

Did not reach for me.

That somehow made it worse.

“What are you?”

She sighed.

“That's becoming a complicated question.”

“Police?”

“No.”

“Military?”

“No.”

“Government?”

“Sometimes adjacent.”

I stared.

She rubbed her forehead.

“Why did you ask about my mother?”

“The church. I was asking about the church.”

She stood carefully.

Her hand trembled once. She tucked the used tissue into her other palm.

“I’m afraid you’re going to have to surrender yourself to the Church for a few weeks.”

I stared.

“What?”

“Temporarily.”

“The Church.”

“Yes.”

“Which church?”

“Technically, yours will be easiest.”

“I just told you I'm not religious.”

“They won’t mind.”

I laughed.

“You’re kidnapping me.”

She winced.

“That is an unnecessarily hostile description.”

“What would you call it?”

“A stay with people who know what to do.”

“That sounds worse.”

“Yes.”

“Are you insane?”

“Possibly, but not about this.”

I started walking away.

She did not stop me.

At the end of the corridor, the elevator stood open.

I froze.

She waited.

I looked at the stairwell.

Then the elevator.

Then at her.

She raised one eyebrow.

“That’s approximately the problem.”

I stayed.

“What happens at the Church?”

“You sleep.”

“What else?”

“You eat.”

I stared.

She thought for a second.

“Three meals a day.”

“That is your sales pitch?”

“The beds are decent.”

“You’re joking.”

“No.”

That was somehow much funnier.

I laughed once.

She continued:

“They'll ask questions. A lot of questions.”

“And if I refuse?”

Her expression changed.

Not threatening.

Sad.

That was worse.

“I strongly recommend that you don't make this complicated.”

“Will they hurt me?”

“I don't expect them to.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No.”

“What will they do to my head?”

She went quiet.

I felt cold.

“What will they do?”

“You'll have to ask them.”

“That sounds like you'll brainwash me.”

She looked away.

“That's a vulgar description.”

“Is it wrong?”

A long pause.

“I can't answer for them.”

I backed away again.

“No.”

“I understand.”

“No, you don't.”

“I probably understand better than you think.”

“You rescued me just to hand me over.”

“Yes.”

The honesty stunned me.

She continued before I could speak.

“I can't leave you outside your office pretending you're going to finish the evening normally.”

“And they can?”

“They can stay.”

“I don't belong to the Church.”

“That has never mattered as much to the Church as people imagine.”

I stared.

She looked exhausted now.

Twenty years older than when she had walked down Floor Forty-Eight.

“They are not going to recruit you.”

“I should be grateful?”

“They are not going to make you clergy.”

“Wonderful.”

“They are not going to kill you.”

I stopped.

She realized immediately that she should not have said that.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You just said they're not going to kill me.”

“I was listing positives.”

“That was on the list?”

She closed her eyes.

“Forget I said that.”

“I cannot forget anything about tonight.”

Her eyes opened again.

“That is precisely the issue.”

Footsteps approached from the far end of the corridor.

Three people.

Two men.

One woman.

Dark civilian clothes.

One of the men wore a clerical collar.

Nothing about him looked like a priest.

The way he scanned doors looked military.

The woman carried a small first-aid case.

The second man looked at the walls, the ceiling, every camera.

“Lynleit,” the man in the collar said.

She looked up.

That was her name, then. I said it once in my head so I would have something to call her if she started to leave.

He looked at the tissue in her hand and the gloves on her belt.

“Bad?”

“It stopped.”

He glanced at me before asking anything else. She shook her head a fraction. Whatever conversation they were having continued without words.

The woman with the first-aid case approached me.

I moved back.

The black-haired woman stepped between us.

“Slowly.”

The woman stopped.

For one second, I understood something else.

They listened to her.

Not completely.

But enough.

“Can I see your hands?” the woman asked.

I held them out. She turned them over, checked a scrape beside my thumb, then asked me to follow her finger with my eyes. She asked my name and where I worked. Those questions I could answer.

“What time do you usually wake up?”

“Half past nine.”

The man in the collar gave a small, almost sympathetic nod.

“Unfortunate. Mass is at eight.”

I looked at Lynleit. She was trying to fasten the gloves to her belt and seemed determined to finish before looking at me.

“We've spoken to your relatives,” the woman said. “They know you're being looked after. You won't have to deal with their calls tonight.”

“My sister?”

“Yes.”

I took out my phone. There were missed calls now. I could not remember giving any of these people my sister's number.

“I want to talk to her.”

“When we're downstairs.”

The man by the cameras had opened the stairwell door. He held it open, looking down rather than at us.

I looked at the black-haired woman.

“Lynleit.”

She looked at me.

It felt strange to have a name for her after all that time following the coat.

“Will I remember you?”

Nobody spoke.

Lynleit looked toward the Church people.

Then back at me.

“I don't know.”

That was the most frightening answer of the night.

I looked at the blood on the tissue.

At the blue coat.

At the corridor.

At the elevator I would never use again.

Then at the people waiting to take me somewhere.

“Will I remember Floor Forty-Seven?”

She thought about it.

“Probably.”

“Will I believe myself?”

Another pause.

“Probably less.”

That should have sounded terrible.

Instead, part of me felt relief.

She noticed.

Of course she did.

The woman offered me her hand.

I did not take it.

I stood by myself.

Then I remembered I still had the coat. I lifted it off my shoulders carefully. Nothing resisted. Nothing moved without my moving it.

Lynleit took it and put it on. Its hem brushed her boots. The gloves disappeared beneath it.

Lynleit gave me a tired little nod.

“That’s better.”

I walked toward the strangers from the Church.

After three steps, I turned.

“Why did you come for me?”

Lynleit looked genuinely confused by the question.

“You were trapped.”

“That’s it?”

“Yes.”

“You're still bleeding.”

She checked the tissue.

“Not now.”

There was a little split where the blood had dried at her lip.

“And that?”

“A few days. It'll heal.”

“You could have died.”

She shrugged.

“Possibly.”

I stared at her.

She seemed suddenly irritated by the conversation.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“Then go.”

I turned away.

Behind me, I heard the man in the collar ask:

“What did you establish?”

Lynleit answered:

“Don't use the numbers.”

“What else?”

“Keep a hand on the rail. Don't trust where it says you're going.”

“Anything else?”

“I'll write it down.”

“You left something in there?”

A pause.

“My pistol.”

That was the last thing I heard before the stairwell door closed.

They had suggested the office elevator.

I refused it.

They took me through the stairs.

Real stairs.

Six floors.

I counted every single landing.

For the next seventeen days, I lived in a monastery that officially did not admit having me.

The beds were, irritatingly, quite good.

There were three meals a day.

Lynleit had not lied about that.

The rest was harder to describe.

Priests who did not speak like priests.

An ordinary doctor who asked about sleep and the scrape on my hand. I began to mention the doors, and the woman who had brought me interrupted to describe an accident at work. Later, when the doctor had gone, the questions about doors resumed.

Men who wanted me to repeat Floor Forty-Seven until it sounded less like a place and more like a stress response.

They never told me magic was not real.

They taught me increasingly sophisticated ways to explain why I should never use that word.

By the time I left, I understood perfectly what had happened to me.

I also understood why saying it aloud would make me sound insane.

Sometimes I wonder whether that was the point.

Sometimes I wonder whether the person who entered that monastery ever actually came back out.

But I returned to work.

Eventually.

I took the stairs.

Always.

Months later, I saw a woman in a long blue coat across a railway platform.

Black hair.

Same posture.

She was looking at her phone.

I stared.

She looked up.

Our eyes met.

For one terrible second I knew her.

Then the train passed between us.

When it was gone, so was she.

I stood there for a long time trying to remember why my heart was beating so hard.

And somewhere inside me, beneath all the explanations the Church had carefully given me, a number surfaced without context.

Forty-seven.
