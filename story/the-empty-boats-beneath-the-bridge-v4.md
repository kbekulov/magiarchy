# The Empty Boats Beneath the Bridge

*The River Choir, Part I. Kyrien.*

## The city side

I checked the other end of the bridge before I checked the ropes.

There was a locked barrier across it. Behind that, a path went into the park and disappeared between trees. The notice on the barrier was large enough to read from the middle of the bridge. It said the area was closed. It didn't say where you should go if you happened to come out of the river on that side.

Reiner found me looking at it.

“Our exit is downstream.”

“I saw.”

“Then check this one.” He put a rope into my hand.

I checked it. He watched until I had finished, took it back, and checked it himself. I didn't object. If I had been responsible for somebody I hadn't chosen, I would have done the same.

The bridge joined the city to the quarantined park. On our side there were houses, a street, steps down to the embankment. A bus passed beyond the gardens often enough that I began recognizing the sound before its lights appeared. On the other side there was the barrier and then nothing lit. You could stand in the middle and hear someone's television through an open window behind you while looking at a place nobody was supposed to enter.

Lynleit had brought me to inspect boats. That was the work, as she explained it. Small wooden craft had been passing at night without lights, and nobody watching from the banks had seen anyone aboard. Drake had information he wouldn't hand over through the usual channels. Sherie had helped persuade Lynleit to supply people instead.

I had seen the envelope change hands at their meeting. I hadn't heard everything said over it. Afterwards Lynleit gave us the part we needed: watch, board, look, leave. We weren't making arrests. We weren't bringing a boat home. We weren't following anything into the park.

“If there's somebody aboard?” I asked.

“Report it before you approach.”

“If they approach us?”

“Then the inspection is over.”

Felix was sitting in the open van, feeding a cable beneath his terminal. He glanced up at that.

“An unusually short assignment. You should enjoy it.”

I asked Lynleit where we would come out.

She showed me the next bridge downstream. Reiner would arrange the lines there before we started. If we couldn't use them, the city embankment had steps. Felix would remain with the van, the cameras and the fallback line. The park was on the wrong side of every part of the plan.

That much was clear. I didn't particularly like having the current choose when we reached the exit, but I could follow the arrangement. I wanted to see the steps for myself. Lynleit came with me while Reiner and the others worked on the ropes.

The lower steps were dark with water. I counted them from the pavement, then went down far enough to see where the wall began to overhang. There was a narrow ledge beside the bottom step. Farther along, there was nowhere obvious to put a foot.

“You can swim?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Reiner will ask you again.”

“He already did.”

She leaned on the upper rail, looking past me at the river. Her usual long coat moved against the iron. A little water had gathered on the rail under her hand, and she wiped it off with her thumb without looking down.

“Is there something you don't like?” she asked.

“These aren't visible from the bridge.”

“Felix will be here if we need them.”

“I mean to somebody in the water.”

She looked at the steps again. This time she came down two of them.

“A light from here,” she said. “Not from the road.”

I nodded.

She stayed a moment longer. I could hear Reiner calling from above. She heard him too, but she was still looking at the water when she answered.

I had agreed to work for her. That didn't mean I thought she told me everything. There were things about my release, the people she could influence, and the work itself that she answered carefully or didn't answer at all. But she had listened about the steps. I found it useful to keep those two observations separate.

## What we did while nothing happened

The first night, nothing came down the river except rubbish.

I watched a pale container approach for so long that I could still see its shape when I looked away. It caught behind one of the bridge supports, turned once and stayed there. Felix asked whether I intended to arrest it. I didn't answer. A few minutes later he asked if it had resisted.

Lynleit ended the watch when the camera window ran out. Reiner recovered the equipment. We drove away without a chase, a discovery, or anything to justify how carefully we had got there.

The second night ended earlier. Felix said his loop was slipping out of time with the traffic lights. I couldn't see the fault until he pointed out the same car passing twice behind a changing reflection. Once he showed me, it was obvious.

“How long?” Reiner asked.

Felix gave him a time.

Lynleit said, “Restore the cameras. We leave.”

Nobody argued for another few minutes. I liked that. It would have been easy to begin treating the limit as negotiable because the boats hadn't obliged us.

Reiner still had work on the bridge, so I waited in the van. My phone had a message confirming that my mother was home and had eaten. I read it twice, sent a reply, and put the phone away before Lynleit climbed in. She took the seat across from me, leaving the side door open.

Felix's cards were in a compartment beside my knee. The box had worn through at one corner. I took them out because there was nothing useful to watch from inside the van and because watching Felix work seemed to annoy him more than was worth the effort.

“Those are mine,” he said.

“I assumed.”

“Don't bend them.”

Several were already bent. I dealt around the worst one.

Lynleit put her gloves beside her on the seat and accepted a hand. We agreed on a short game. No money. Felix said he wanted a share if there was money. Lynleit told him to finish what he was doing.

I won. She looked at the last card I had put down, then at my hand, as though the part she wanted to examine might still be there.

“Again.”

Reiner shut the van door before we could deal.

She remembered on the third night. I hadn't thought she would forget, but I was pleased she brought it up first.

That watch had been worse than the others without giving us anything we could use. A branch broke inside the park. I turned towards it before the wind reached the trees along the water. Lynleit turned a moment after me. We both stood looking through the barrier, listening to the leaves begin moving. It could have been rotten timber. It could have been somebody stepping where the ground gave way. Nothing followed it.

“What was that?” she asked.

“A branch.”

“Yes, thank you.”

She waited. I had nothing better to give her.

We had no reason to cross the barrier. We left it where it was and watched for boats. They didn't come. By the time we got back into the van, I still wanted to know why I had turned so quickly.

Lynleit sat opposite me and tapped the cards.

For the first few hands she watched my fingers more than the cards on the shelf between us.

“You have cards of your own,” I said.

She looked down at them, as though checking my claim, and carried on watching me.

I won that game too. On the last play I took a risk I didn't need to take. I remember that because she moved her attention from my hand to my face just before I put the card down. She didn't say anything. I thought she had noticed the card I was protecting. I began keeping my hand flatter.

On the fourth night, Felix had a pale mark on one of the camera panes.

It was near the park entrance. Not the whole shape of a person. A narrow interruption beside the path, too light against the trees. He went back over the loop and found it again.

“Compression,” he said.

“Show us the live feed,” Lynleit said.

He did. The path was empty.

I looked from the screen to the barrier outside. There wasn't a white sign in that position. I asked how old the loop was. Felix told me, then told me that an image defect didn't become a visitor just because I disliked it.

“I didn't say it was a visitor.”

“You were preparing to.”

Lynleit asked him to keep the frame. She didn't send anyone into the park. When the watch ended, she gave the same order about the cameras and sat down to finish our rematch.

I had a chance to end it quickly. She watched me consider the play. Then she smiled and put down an ordinary card.

I looked at her for longer than I should have. Not long. Long enough to lose the count I had been keeping.

I played the safer card.

“You changed the conditions,” I said.

“The cards are unchanged.”

I counted again from what I could see. She waited with the same small smile, enjoying the wait. I wanted to beat her rather more than the game deserved.

After that I watched her hands. She moved her thumb along the edge of a card before committing herself. It wasn't every move, and when I thought I had found an easy way to read her, I lost a hand. I had mistaken impatience for a decision. She laid the winning card down and looked at me until I collected it.

Felix reached between us for a lead he needed. We both moved our cards out of his way without stopping. He looked from her to me, unplugged the lead and went back to his screen.

We were still playing when Reiner returned.

On the fifth night there was a hand left unfinished on the shelf. Mine were face down. Hers were opposite. Felix called us out before either of us could make the other finish.

## The fifth watch

The drive had been clear. On two earlier nights fog had slowed us before we reached the river, but this time I could see the houses at the top of the slope. Below the bridge it was different. The mist lay in broad stretches over the water. A reflection would disappear in it and return farther down with no lamp visible above.

I checked the steps again. Felix's line was ready. Reiner made me repeat which bank I would use if I couldn't regain a boat.

“City side,” I said.

“Even if the park looks closer.”

I looked across. Roots projected over the water there. They were easier to see than the steps.

Lynleit wore a short blue suede jacket that night. I noticed because I had been expecting her to lift the long coat clear of the ropes. She tested the reach of both arms before settling against the rail. The holster at her lower back stayed covered when she bent.

“If they do not appear tonight, we end the pattern,” she said.

“Because the operation has failed?”

“Because repeating an intrusion after the target has noticed it is not surveillance.”

Felix said something about that being directed at someone. I let him have it. I was looking at the fixed reflection under the bridge. There was another reflection beside it that hadn't been there before.

Reiner raised his hand.

A boat came out of the mist. Then another. Then a third, close enough behind the second that I first took its bow for something tied to the stern.

They were low wooden craft with dark tarps over the holds. No lights, no visible load. The first turned a little in the current, corrected, and passed under a pale patch from the bridge lamp. I could see the rudder held in position. I couldn't see anyone controlling it.

Felix looked away from his terminal towards the real water.

“Those are our boats.”

“I can see them,” Reiner said.

“There is nobody aboard.”

“That is what we are here to establish.”

I watched the gap between the first two boats. It widened under the support and narrowed again beyond it. They weren't tied together. At least there was no line above the water. If there was someone beneath the tarp, they were willing to come under the bridge without looking where they were going.

Lynleit leaned over far enough to check beneath our ropes. She looked back at Reiner. He nodded.

“Positions,” she said.

He went first. His descent made the boat look steadier than I expected. He got his feet down near its centre, bent his knees, and let the line slide clear. Maren and Ivo followed onto the second boat. Lynleit waited for the third.

I stood back from the rail.

“You first.”

“Is that concern?”

“If I go first, Felix may cut the rope.”

His answer arrived through the earpiece. “The possibility has been evaluated.”

She went over without answering him. I watched her find the boat and followed.

From above, the boards had looked merely wet. With my weight on them they shifted and the whole boat rolled. Lynleit was still moving her foot clear of the descent line. Her shoulder went back. I caught her at the waist, and the heel of my hand struck the hard leather edge beneath her jacket.

She caught the rail. I let go.

For a moment I was aware of exactly where my hand had been. Then the rope slid out of reach and we were moving away from the bridge.

## Under the tarps

There was nobody at the stern. I checked there first, although there was nowhere for a person to hide except under the covering we could both see.

Lynleit drew the tarp back. It sagged into the hold. I expected boxes low enough to be covered without showing a shape, or someone lying flat with a weapon pointed up at us. There was nothing. A little water, ribs of timber, the underside of the boards.

“Empty,” Reiner said from the first boat.

Ivo answered from the second. The same.

Felix asked us to check underneath.

I crouched while Lynleit held the tarp clear. One of the boards lifted at the edge. I got my fingers beneath it and raised it far enough to look. Water moved below. I lowered it carefully; it would have been a stupid place to trap a hand.

“Anything?” she asked.

“Water.”

She looked herself. I moved so she could get the angle without leaning over me.

The boats still looked as though somebody had prepared them to carry something. The alterations were useful ones: a hold you could cover quickly, nothing projecting high enough to catch the eye from the street. I could understand a crew hiding before a bridge. I couldn't understand why they would cover nothing and then abandon all three craft in the same stretch of current.

I looked back upstream. The bridge was receding behind the mist. There wasn't a swimmer following us that I could see.

“How long until the next bridge?” Lynleit asked.

Felix told her. Reiner said we would be ready for the lines.

I checked the side facing the park. Low branches covered the bank, and the mist made them look detached from their trunks. The nearer ones were visible against the water. Beyond them there was only a grey darkness where the path ought to be.

Something held my attention there. I stopped breathing to listen.

Lynleit looked at me before I turned my head fully.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

I meant nothing I could point to. She didn't like the answer. Neither did I. I kept looking for a break in the movement of the leaves.

Maren spoke from the next boat.

“There is something beside us.”

Reiner told her not to lean out.

She had already moved. I saw her shoulder above the tarp, her hand on the side. Then her hip struck the wood and her foot was gone.

I didn't see anything rise to take it.

Reiner crossed the gap while it was still narrow. He caught her wrist and went down on one knee. She was hanging against the side, trying to bring her other leg back into the boat. For a second he held her there.

“Line.”

Ivo threw it. Lynleit told him where to brace. I grabbed the loose portion that came across our deck before it could slide beneath my foot.

Maren said something was holding her legs.

I thought of a submerged cable. I looked for tension in the water, a snag pulling her behind the moving hull. There wasn't one I could identify. Her body went down almost straight beneath her hands.

Reiner's shoulder jerked. The boat rolled towards him. Ivo pulled from the other side, but the first boat was already moving away without anybody on it. The distance between us changed while they were trying to use it.

Lynleit was giving directions beside me. I saw black gloves and her sleeves pushed back. I didn't ask about them. Reiner went in, and Maren disappeared beneath the side of the boat.

Ivo entered the water after them.

The loose line slid over our gunwale. I had it in my hand when the next pull came. It ran across Lynleit's boot and drew tight around a fitting. The boat began to turn towards the pull.

I had my knife out before I could decide whether I was cutting away something we would need. There wasn't time to untie it. I cut the line beside the fitting, and the freed length went into the river.

The boat rolled back under us. Its side hit my thigh. I reached for wood and found the wet tarp instead.

## The nearer ground

I went in with my mouth open. I remember being angry about that before I understood anything else. I had seen it coming and still hadn't kept my mouth shut.

The cold made me draw up my knees. My jacket dragged across my shoulders. I pushed it away from my throat, kicked and found air.

Felix was saying, “City side. Do not fight for the boats. City side.”

I turned towards the lamps. Reiner had an arm under Maren's shoulders. Ivo was close to him. I could see them in pieces, a head, a raised forearm, water across someone's face. They were making progress towards the steps.

I tried to follow. Something closed around my ankle.

It felt like a hand. I didn't want to decide that while I was swallowing water. I kicked down, expecting whatever had snagged me to loosen if I changed the angle. The pressure moved higher instead. There was a separate pressure against the back of my knee.

I went under again.

I couldn't see anything useful. My eyes stung, and the lights above had become long pale strokes. I brought my other foot against the grip, kicked and turned. I was free for one stroke. That stroke took me farther from the city.

When I got my head up, Lynleit's boat was on its side. I saw her face beside it, then an arm reaching past the boards. I tried to call her name. Water broke against my mouth before I could finish.

I could have spent the rest of my strength swimming towards where she had been. I tried for long enough to find that I wasn't getting closer. Something pulled beneath my left leg. My shoulders turned while I was still trying to go straight.

I stopped fighting for the lamps and looked the other way.

The roots on the park bank were nearer. I had noticed them from the bridge because they projected over the water. Now I could distinguish the one that forked near its end. Behind it was mud, a slope, trees. I couldn't see anything waiting there. I also couldn't see anything holding my leg.

I kicked towards the root.

For two strokes I moved. On the third my leg stopped again and my face went under. I bent towards it, got a hand against my own trouser leg, and found fingers through the cloth. I couldn't get a useful grip on them. I drove my heel across them and kicked for the pale fork I had last seen above me.

I came up short of it. My first reach caught leaves. They tore away in my hand.

I let the current take me a little farther, though every part of me wanted to go straight at the bank. The thicker root was downstream of the leaves. I reached again, caught it with my fingertips and got my other hand over it before I slipped.

There was no bottom beneath my feet. I had both arms bent, my chest against the root, and nothing to push on. I tried to pull higher and nearly lost the hold.

Across the water a light moved down the steps. Felix shouted a name I couldn't make out. Somebody answered. The sound gave me enough of an ordinary direction to stop trying everything at once.

I put my right hand farther along the root. It was thicker there. I pulled until I could get an elbow over it, then brought my knee up towards the mud. Something dragged at my shoe. I kicked without looking down.

This time my knee found ground.

I pressed it into the bank and pulled again.
