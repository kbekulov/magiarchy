# Chapter 03: After the Failed Attempt

## The invoices

The lawyer had put the old statement beside the copy used in the prosecution. Kyrien could see the difference without understanding the notes in the margins.

"That isn't what he said."

"No."

He looked at her. She had been careful with him for so long that the unqualified answer took a moment to register.

"Can you prove that?"

"We have the original, the amendment, and the record of who received each. This one doesn't depend on anybody remembering correctly."

Kyrien rubbed a mark on his cuff. He had come straight from work; there had been nowhere to wash properly, and the mark was dry now.

"And the other allegations?"

She brought out another folder.

There had always been another allegation. His father would answer one, sometimes with records from the very office accusing him, and a fresh question would arrive before the answer had done any good. By the end, even people who believed him asked why he could not leave it alone.

After his death, the papers had supplied an answer for him. Shame. Instability. Fear of what might be uncovered next.

Kyrien had paid people to examine those answers. He took extra shifts, found work between them, and learned which expenses could wait. Each month he put his mother's bills beside the solicitor's account and worked out what was left. Sometimes the solicitor's account waited too. She continued answering his calls.

He brought documents, remembered who had visited the house, corrected a date when he knew it was wrong. When she explained an application he did not understand, he asked her to explain it again. The legal work was hers.

Now an independent inquiry had accepted enough of it to examine the senior prosecutor.

"The preliminary review supports us," she said. "Important evidence was knowingly distorted. That is what they have found."

On the way home, Kyrien bought the tea his mother preferred. He had been buying the cheaper one and telling himself she had not noticed.

She noticed this one immediately.

"Was it on offer?"

"No."

She turned the packet over, checking anyway.

For a while, the folder on his kitchen table contained something other than a bill.

## The closed review

The lawyer did not put the two statements in front of him this time.

"They haven't disproved them," she said.

Kyrien waited.

"The review went into closed session. The prosecutor's conduct has been accepted under a confidential national-security mandate. We cannot challenge what we are not permitted to examine."

"They examined it."

"Yes."

"And they have our evidence."

"Yes."

She went through the remaining routes with him. He wrote down the first two, then stopped when she explained why neither could reach the decision that mattered. She let him keep the pen.

He asked for copies of everything they were allowed to retain.

The prosecutor's vindication was public. So was the suggestion that a malicious campaign had tried to ruin an honourable man. The altered statement stayed in the folder.

One of his father's old contacts met him afterward. Kyrien had trusted him enough to bring the papers.

"Your father was wronged," the man said quietly.

Kyrien pushed the folder toward him.

The man did not open it.

"There are consequences this country cannot afford."

"For whom?"

He received no useful answer. He took the folder home because the copies had cost money.

He continued working. His mother still needed meals, clean clothes, company on the bad days, and to be left alone on the days she could manage. There were mornings when she had already washed the cups before he got up. There were evenings when she sat in the same chair until he asked whether she had eaten.

He kept paying what he owed the lawyer. For a time, he still called.

## The shopping

His mother wanted to go to the shop herself.

Kyrien offered to carry the bag. She considered that, then gave him the heavier one and kept the small cloth bag she always used for bread.

Inside, she checked the tea before putting it back. At the counter she was counting change when a customer recognized her.

"Aren't you the commander's wife?"

Her hand stopped over the coins.

"Yes."

"I saw they cleared the prosecutor. Terrible business. All that trouble, and for what? Compensation?"

Kyrien took the bag from the counter.

"We're leaving."

His mother did not move.

"My husband didn't... The evidence wasn't..."

"Well, they've looked at it, haven't they?"

The shopkeeper glanced at the people waiting behind her.

"Please. There's no need to make a scene."

He was speaking to her.

One customer watched. Another studied the shelves behind the till. Kyrien gathered the loose coins, paid, and stood beside his mother until she would take his arm.

Outside she tried to open the bread bag. Her fingers kept missing the fold.

"I'm sorry. I thought I could manage the shopping."

"You can."

"I shouldn't have answered."

He held the bag open for her.

At home she wanted the bread put away before she sat down. He did that, made her tea, and found the programme she had missed. When she asked whether he was going to work, he told her the time of his shift.

Only after she had settled did he take the prosecutor's photograph out of the folder.

He meant to kill him.

His mother would still need someone to come home. He knew that while he looked at the photograph, and afterward, when he put it away and left for work.

He arranged for a neighbour his mother already knew to stay during his longer absences and left her number beside the telephone. His mother complained that neither of them could make tea properly and then asked when the neighbour was coming again.

## The appointment

The senior prosecutor became Prosecutor General. A formal dinner marked the appointment; surviving the supposed smear campaign was mentioned as a recommendation for the office.

MSF supplied part of the security. Lynleit attended, spoke civilly to a man she disliked, and was almost harmed when Kyrien's attempt failed.

Kyrien got away. His father's old contacts and the practical knowledge he had inherited had helped him prepare, but something at the dinner had escaped his understanding. He had seen nothing he could identify as magic.

He went back to work.

The police came later. His history supplied a motive. The incident supplied questions. They did not yet have the evidence to turn their certainty into a conclusive case.

## The interview

Kyrien had counted the screws in the table bracket twice when the door opened.

The woman who came in carried a slim file. She did not ask where to sit. His lawyer pulled her own chair a little closer to his.

"Lynleit. MSF. We assisted with security at the dinner."

The investigating officer introduced her for the record. His lawyer asked what part of the questioning required the contractor's presence, listened to the answer, and made a note.

"You can ask," she said. "He may decline to answer."

Lynleit sat opposite him.

Her file was much thinner than his father's had been.

"Did you follow the appointment?"

"It was on the news."

"You had a particular interest."

"So did the news."

She waited. He looked at the officer, then back at her.

"You understand why we're speaking to you."

"My lawyer explained."

"Your complaint against the prosecutor was dismissed."

"The evidence wasn't."

For the first time she opened the file.

"No," she said. "It wasn't."

He did not help her with the silence that followed.

She asked about his work, the dinner, and people who had known his father. When a question moved from what he knew to where he had been, his lawyer touched the edge of her notebook.

"I won't answer that."

Lynleit looked at the notebook. Then she asked whether he understood how many people had been working at the dinner.

"Catering staff. Drivers. Security. People who had no part in your father's case."

"Is that a question?" his lawyer asked.

"Did he give any thought to them?"

"You're asking him to accept your account of his involvement."

Lynleit shut the file with one hand. Not loudly, but before the lawyer had finished speaking.

Kyrien looked at that hand.

"I didn't say I was there."

"I heard you."

"Then ask me something I can answer."

She watched him for long enough that he began to wish he had left the last sentence out.

Then she drew out a payment schedule.

"These are yours?"

His lawyer checked it before he did.

"Copies we supplied with the complaint," she said. "To answer the allegation that he was financing it with somebody else's money."

Lynleit placed the employment records beside it.

"You changed shifts here. Took a second job here. This payment arrived in three parts."

"Four," Kyrien said.

She found the small entry at the bottom.

"Four."

He had forgotten that one. He remembered it now: getting home too late, his mother still awake, the envelope under a plate so he would not leave it behind in the morning.

"You were still paying your lawyers after the review."

"They were still answering."

His lawyer lowered her eyes to the schedule.

"Then something changed."

Kyrien looked at the table.

"What happened?"

He rubbed his thumb along the seam of his cuff. The shift he had missed would have paid the neighbour and most of the next bill.

"They started on my mother."

"Who did?"

"A man in a shop."

Lynleit looked up from the papers.

He had given her the wrong sort of answer. She had been ready for a name, perhaps another official. He could see her waiting for him to supply one.

"She was buying bread," he said.

The lawyer did not interrupt. He told them about the coins, the shopkeeper, and getting his mother outside. He stopped before repeating what she had said to him.

"And after that?" Lynleit asked.

"I took her home."

"Did you..."

"I took her home."

She left the question unfinished.

"Is someone with her now?"

"Our neighbour. Until six."

The answer came too quickly. He sat back.

"Why?"

"Because it's nearly six."

She looked at his lawyer. "Can you find out whether the neighbour can stay?"

The officer agreed to a break, and the lawyer used the number Kyrien had left with her.

The neighbour could stay. His mother had eaten. She wanted to know whether he had his key.

"Yes," he said, although nobody on the telephone could hear him.

## What Lynleit knew

Lynleit read the review heading again while the lawyer spoke quietly to her client.

She knew the sort of restricted authority that could keep a witness out of court without ever calling him a witness. The file before her had begun with a commander who survived an arrest gone wrong, then filled with allegations that changed whenever he answered them. Its last pages protected the people who had arranged the case.

Suppression. The word belonged to reports she was permitted to read and Kyrien was not.

She turned over one of his receipts. The late charge was smaller than the price of her lunch.

She could still remember where she had been standing at the dinner, and how little had separated her from the attack. Disliking the prosecutor had been easier before that.

Across the table, Kyrien asked when he could go home.

Lynleit could recommend a release arrangement. She could not make the evidence disappear, and she could not promise what the police would do with evidence they might find tomorrow.

She also could not shake the thought that she would need him.

Helena knew her officers, her friends, the people Fionn trusted. Recently she had begun asking about visits and journeys that had never interested her before. Lynleit had explanations for each question. None explained why she kept remembering them together.

She asked the investigating officer for a separate discussion with Kyrien and his representative once the recorded interview was over.

"About what?"

"Work."

## The offer

The lawyer stayed. Kyrien had expected Lynleit to object; instead she put a different sheet between them and waited while the lawyer read it.

The door was closed. The formal questioning had ended. Nothing she offered here would erase the record they had already made.

"MSF can request an arrangement under its government contract," Lynleit said. "I can put you forward for it. That means work, supervision, and a case that remains open. It doesn't mean I own the police."

"Does your father?" Kyrien asked.

"He owns MSF."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the answer you're getting."

His lawyer turned the sheet over.

"Who approves release?"

"The responsible authorities. Not me. I can recommend it, provide the undertaking, and argue that continued detention serves no useful purpose."

"And if he declines your employment?"

"You continue pursuing his release without our support."

"Which I will," the lawyer told him.

Lynleit nodded, once.

Kyrien looked at the sheet. She was not offering him a clean name. She had brought a way to get home sooner and made herself part of it.

"What work?"

"Watching. Checking things. Telling me when an explanation doesn't fit what you've seen. Sometimes going somewhere without advertising who sent you."

"You have people for that."

"People know who they are."

"And you think nobody knows who I am?"

"They know whose son you are. They don't know you work for me."

"I don't."

"Not yet."

His lawyer made a mark beside a paragraph.

"Payment?"

Lynleit gave her the employment terms. The lawyer asked another question, and then a third. Kyrien waited until the figures were on paper before doing the arithmetic.

"This has to replace the shifts I lose," he said. "Not be paid whenever you decide the work was useful."

"Regular pay. Expenses separately."

"My mother can't stay alone for days."

"Then before you're away, someone she knows has to be able to stay. Put the cost in the expenses."

"She chooses who."

"Yes."

"And if nobody can?"

Lynleit looked at him rather than the lawyer.

"You tell me before you go. We change the assignment."

He had answers to the questions he had expected to fight about. He trusted them less for arriving so readily.

"What happens when you don't need me?"

"If the arrangement works, I'll help you get clear of the scrutiny you can actually get clear of. I won't promise immunity."

"Can I leave?"

His lawyer put her pen down.

"We'll need that in writing. Along with the release conditions, who can change them, and what happens if employment ends."

"Agreed," Lynleit said.

Kyrien looked at her. "You haven't answered."

"You can leave the work. I can't promise that every protection attached to it survives your leaving."

His lawyer kept the sheet on her side of the table.

"He will not sign this version. Bring the complete terms. I review them, and I remain his representative."

"Of course."

"And no questions about the dinner disguised as an assignment."

Lynleit's mouth tightened.

"Understood."

Kyrien had watched officials agree pleasantly before. He looked at the written rate again, then at the clock.

"I'll do the work," he said. "If the terms say what you've said."

"They will."

"My lawyer checks."

"I heard her."

Lynleit gathered the papers she had brought. His lawyer kept the proposed terms, with the unsigned page folded to the front.

Before leaving, Lynleit asked whether he had any other questions.

"Can I speak to my mother myself?"

She went to ask the officer.

## The people she told

Later, Felix stood with the back of a chair between his hands while Reiner read the short account Lynleit had given them.

"You recruited him."

"Yes."

"The man from the dinner."

"There was more than one man at the dinner."

"Lynleit."

She stopped sorting the papers.

"I need someone Helena hasn't already counted."

"She hasn't counted plenty of people. Most haven't tried to kill somebody beside you."

Reiner looked up. "Does he know you were there?"

"No."

"Your father?"

"Doesn't know about the arrangement. And won't hear it from us."

Felix let go of the chair.

Reiner set the account down. "You trust him?"

"No. I think he'll do the work."

He considered that for a while.

"All right."

Felix turned toward him. "That's all?"

"I trust her judgment. It doesn't mean we stop watching."

"Good," Felix said. "Because I wasn't going to."

Lynleit slid the account back into its folder.
