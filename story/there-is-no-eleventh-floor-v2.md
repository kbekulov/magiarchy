# There Is No Eleventh Floor

I'd worked in that building for six years. My office was on the sixth floor, and there were ten floors altogether, with one parking level underneath. I mention that because, afterward, people kept asking whether I could have mistaken where I was. There were plenty of things I couldn't explain to them. The number of floors wasn't one of them.

I was leaving late that evening. The elevator arrived at 22:49, I stepped inside and pressed **1**, and while the doors closed I checked a message from my sister.

*You alive?*

*Leaving now,* I wrote.

I wasn't paying much attention to the display at first. It was counting down, and I could feel the little vibration the elevator always made between floors. When it reached **1**, I put my phone away and moved toward the doors. They didn't open. I waited for the usual jolt as it stopped, but we were still going down.

Then the display showed **0**.

I thought someone must have called it from the parking level. I didn't like having to go down and come back up, but that was all I thought about it. Then it showed **-1**, followed by **-2**, and I reached across to press **1** again. There wasn't a second level below the building. I looked at the buttons to make sure there wasn't something I'd never noticed in six years.

The light around **1** was already on. Pressing it again did nothing. Neither did Door Open. At **-3**, I tried the alarm and kept my finger on it, waiting for a voice from the speaker. By **-5**, I had my phone out again. No signal. I turned toward the doors with it, as if that would help.

“Come on,” I said.

The display reached **-8**. Something scraped across the roof of the elevator.

I stopped pressing the alarm to listen. The scraping stopped too. For perhaps ten seconds I stood there with my thumb just above the button. I didn't know whether I wanted to hear it again. If it was machinery, there ought to have been another sound, a motor or something shifting back into place.

Instead, a weight settled on the ceiling panel. The panel began to bow toward me. The light came down with it, exposing a strip of clean metal along its edge, and dust fell onto the screen of my phone.

I moved into the corner beside the buttons. There wasn't anywhere else to go. My shoulder pressed the alarm while I watched the ceiling, and I kept waiting for someone to answer. The button was getting warm through my jacket.

One dent appeared in the panel. Then four more came down beside it. They were arranged like fingers, and once I'd seen that I couldn't make them look like anything else.

At **-14**, the elevator stopped and the doors opened onto my office.

I could see the grey carpet, the beige walls and the framed evacuation plan opposite the doors. I knew that plan. I'd walked past it twice a day for years. For a moment I thought the display had been broken all along and we hadn't gone anywhere.

But the ceiling was still coming down. I looked from it to the corridor, trying to decide whether to get out. The doors stayed open. One of the dents deepened, and I ran through them.

I heard the doors close behind me. When I turned, there was only a plaster wall.

I went straight back and hit it with both hands. I felt along the middle, then lower down, looking for the seam where the doors had met. My nails picked up grit. I found a shallow scratch and kept working at it before I realized I'd made it myself.

“Hello? There's somebody here. I'm stuck.”

The last word came back from the kitchen. I shouted again, louder this time, and then put my ear against the wall. I wanted to hear the scraping. A few seconds earlier I'd wanted to get as far from it as possible. Now I needed something on the other side to tell me the elevator was still there.

Nobody answered. I kept calling until my throat hurt, then decided to find the stairs. Whatever had happened to the lift, I could walk down six floors.

The office looked convincing while I was standing still. It was walking through it that went wrong. I passed Meeting Room C, the kitchen and reception, and then found myself outside Meeting Room C again. I hadn't turned a corner that could have taken me back there.

I retraced my steps. There was the room again, with the same sign. I went a little farther, looking for some difference in the doors, but by then I wasn't sure which one I'd started from.

I put my phone on the carpet outside one of them. Then I walked toward the kitchen without taking my eyes off it. Twelve steps. The phone stayed where I'd put it. From the kitchen, though, I could see another Meeting Room C farther along the corridor.

There was a phone outside that one too.

I went back for mine. I kept looking at the one I was picking up because I didn't want to see what happened beside the other door when I did it. I don't know why that seemed important. I just wanted my phone back in my hand.

After that I noticed the mugs. Every desk had the same one. The computers were all showing black screens, and the wall clock said **02:17**. My phone said **22:53**. I checked it twice before putting it away.

When I finally found the stairwell, I was so relieved by the concrete steps and green handrail that I went through without stopping to look above me. They were stairs. I could feel the rail under my hand, and I could see the next landing. I started climbing, thinking that if the elevator really had taken me below ground I ought to go up.

I counted the landings aloud. At the third one, the door said **FLOOR 22**.

“No,” I said, and went back down a flight.

The next door said **23**. The one below it said **24**.

I stopped looking at the signs and counted steps instead. Sixteen to a landing, then another sixteen. I kept one hand on the rail so I wouldn't accidentally turn back on myself. When I opened the next door, there was dark green wallpaper on the other side, a child's bicycle and a row of apartment doors.

There had never been apartments in the building.

I shut it. Three knocks came from the other side, and a little girl said, “Wrong floor.”

I nearly answered her. I had my mouth open to ask which floor I should be on. Then I remembered the bicycle, and the wallpaper where an office ought to have been, and I went down the stairs as fast as I could.

After that I tried not to answer voices behind doors. I wish I could say I followed that rule consistently, but when I heard someone crying in a bathroom, I went in.

The woman was in one of the stalls. She said she was lost too. I can't explain how much I wanted that to be true. I didn't need her to know the way out. Having somebody else there would have been enough.

I moved closer and looked under the door. Her heels faced me and her toes pointed toward the toilet, which was ordinary enough. It was the voice I couldn't account for. It came from just above the latch, close enough to stir a loose strip of paper stuck there.

“Please,” she said.

I bent a little, trying to see whether there was another person in the stall. The shoes stayed where they were. Neither foot shifted when the voice came lower, nearer to where I'd put my face.

“I can see you.”

I backed away. The crying stopped, and the stall door began to open. I didn't wait to see the rest of her.

I started writing warnings on my forearm after that. **DO NOT FOLLOW CRYING** was one. There were others: **RED DOORS LIE**, **CHECK CEILING FIRST**, **DO NOT TRUST FLOOR NUMBERS**. I kept pulling my sleeve back to read them because I was afraid I'd forget something important as soon as I reached another door.

At some point I found **WOMAN IN WHITE IS NOT HUMAN** written there too.

I couldn't remember writing it. I couldn't remember meeting a woman in white. I tried to work backward from the bathroom, but I couldn't put all the corridors in order anymore. The warning was in my handwriting. I left it alone.

For a while I'd been using my phone to keep track of how long I walked. Then its clock changed to **02:17**. I watched it, waiting for the minute to turn. I counted breaths, lost count and started again. There was a wall clock nearby with a moving second hand, so I waited for that instead. When it reached the top, it clicked backward one mark.

The battery on my phone said **73%**. However often I checked, it stayed at seventy-three. I stopped being able to say how long I'd been there. It felt like hours, but I couldn't have proved that to anyone.

I came out into a parking garage that was supposedly fifty floors below ground. It had windows showing daylight. I could even smell warm cars, and I went toward the glass because I thought I might at least be able to see which side of the building I was on.

There was a strip of sky and the tops of some trees outside. I crouched, keeping my palm on the window. The view didn't change. I stood on tiptoe, but I couldn't see any farther down or up. The trees stayed in exactly the same place against the frame.

Behind the parked cars, an engine tried to start. I couldn't see which car it was. It turned over once, stopped, then tried again. I backed toward the stairwell, and the engine stopped as soon as I touched the handle. I went through before it could start again.

There was an office with employee photographs that showed nothing but empty chairs. I stood close enough to one to check that I wasn't missing a person in the background. Then I moved on. In another corridor, the walls got closer together as I walked. I thought it was the angle at first, until one shoulder brushed the wall and then the other did too. I had to back out with my elbows held against me.

I don't know how many doors I passed before I found one with my apartment number on it.

I didn't open it. I stood there looking at the number, and then my mother called my name from inside.

She'd been dead nine years.

I sat down with my bag between my feet and brought up my sister's number. There was still no signal, but I held my thumb over the call button anyway.

“You've left your bag,” my mother said.

I looked down at it. I could remember her saying that, though not on any particular day. It was such an ordinary thing for her to say that for a moment I wanted to answer that I had it, that it was right here.

I put the phone away. I couldn't keep looking at my sister's name while I listened to our mother's voice.

Then something inside the apartment said, “You know I'm not her.”

I got up so quickly I nearly left the bag behind. I ran until I found another stairwell, and there I sat on a landing marked **47** and began laughing. I couldn't seem to stop. Forty-seven floors in a building with ten, and I was still carrying my bag as if I were going home from work.

That was where the woman found me.

I heard her footsteps first. They came down from above, with enough time between them for me to sit still and listen. There was no dragging or clicking. I wanted to call out, but I'd wanted to answer the woman in the bathroom too, so I waited.

A black boot appeared around the turn of the staircase, then the other. They laced above her knees. As she came down, her long blue coat opened enough for me to see dark tights and fitted black shorts. She had a pale blouse buttoned high at the neck, a narrow black ribbon and very long dark hair that had fallen over one shoulder.

She looked young, perhaps in her twenties. She stopped a few steps above me. Her gaze went to my hands, my shoes, the writing on my arm, then past me into the stairwell.

“You're alive.”

I stood up to ask her who she was and nearly fell. She caught my shoulder.

“Easy.”

“Who are you?”

“Can you walk?”

I asked who she was again. She asked whether I could walk again. I finally said yes, because I was afraid she might leave if I didn't answer.

“Good.”

She looked at the number on the landing, then put her hand on the concrete beside it. She knocked twice, listened and knocked again. I watched her face, waiting for something that would tell me she knew where we were.

“Forty-eight isn't real,” I said.

“I know.”

“You came from there.”

“Yes.”

“You know this place?”

“No.”

I didn't know what to do with that. I'd already begun thinking of her as someone who could get me out, and she was telling me she didn't know the building either.

She crouched beside the landing edge and ran two fingers through the dust. There were footprints all over it, going in both directions. They looked like mine. I checked the soles of my shoes against the nearest print, then looked farther along the landing. There were dozens.

“I haven't been here before.”

“Probably.”

“What does that mean?”

“Don't worry about it yet.”

She took a small metal object from her coat. I thought it was a weapon until she held it between her fingers. It was a coin. She dropped it down the stairs and we listened to it striking the steps: three quick clinks, then a pause long enough for me to think it had stopped.

The next clink came from above us.

We both looked up. Her expression tightened.

“What?” I asked.

“We don't go down.”

“Why?”

“The coin did.”

“That doesn't make sense.”

“No.”

She kept looking up the staircase. I couldn't tell what she'd learned, but she had stopped studying the lower flight. When she began to climb, I caught her sleeve.

“Up?”

“Yes.”

“I've been going down for hours.”

“That's probably why you're on forty-seven.”

She looked at my hand on her coat, and I loosened it. I didn't quite let go.

“You don't have to understand it,” she said.

“I would like to.”

“So would I.”

Something moved beneath the stairs. She put a finger to her lips before I could ask another question.

A hand curled around the underside of the flight below us. Then a second one appeared, with long grey fingers that gripped the concrete from underneath. I pulled my feet back. The woman stayed where she was, watching them.

There was a yellow safety stripe along the landing edge. The hands moved sideways beneath it but didn't come across. When she stepped back, they followed as far as the stripe. She stepped forward and they withdrew.

“What are you doing?” I whispered.

“Learning.”

There was a wet clicking sound under the stairs. She picked up a loose bolt and tossed it beyond the yellow paint. The hands shot toward it so quickly I flinched into the wall.

“Stay inside the paint,” she said.

“What?”

“Inside the yellow lines.”

“Why?”

“Because it hasn't crossed them.”

“That's it?”

“That's enough.”

I would have liked a better reason. I watched the stripe while we crossed the landing anyway. The hands followed beneath the next flight, close enough for me to hear them grip and release, but they didn't come onto the marked part where we stood.

We passed **48**, then **12**, then **9**, then **67**. I kept noticing the numbers even after I'd decided not to trust them. At nine I thought we might be close to the top of the real building. Sixty-seven took that away again.

She wasn't looking at them.

“How do you know where we're going?” I asked.

“I don't.”

I slowed down. She looked back and added, “But the numbers are part of the trap.”

“How do you know?”

“Because they want you to react to them.”

She pointed along the handrail.

“Concrete stays concrete. Handrail stays continuous. Gravity still works most of the time. Air is moving upward through the shaft. Trust things the place has to maintain. Not the things it wants you to read.”

I put my hand back on the rail. I didn't understand what she'd said about gravity working most of the time, and I didn't want to ask. I could follow a handrail. For the next flight I concentrated on doing that.

At a door marked **6**, I nearly went past her.

“That's my floor.”

She stopped before touching the handle.

“Which way does your stairwell door open?”

“What?”

“Normally.”

I had to picture myself arriving at work before I could answer. “Out.”

“This opens in.”

She stepped away from it. The handle began turning by itself, and someone on the other side whispered, “You found it.”

She reached behind her waist and drew a pistol. It was small, with a short silver slide and a dark grip almost covered by her hand. Seeing it reassured me for a moment. I knew what a gun was supposed to do. It was the first thing she'd produced that I thought I understood.

She fired twice through the door. Something screamed on the other side. I couldn't tell whether it had moved away or was coming toward us. She caught my wrist and said, “Run.”

We went up. I didn't argue about the direction that time.

The next door took us into an office corridor where every room was numbered **1004**. Partway along, she turned me around and made me walk backward. My heel caught the carpet almost immediately. She caught my sleeve before I fell.

“Small steps.”

I kept watching the door we'd entered through. It should have been getting farther away, but each time I blinked it seemed a little closer. Behind me she was counting under her breath. Her hand stayed at my elbow, moving me back into line when I drifted toward the wall.

I wanted to turn around. Walking backward without knowing what was behind me was bad enough; having that door creep toward me made it much worse. I kept my head facing it until my neck hurt.

At thirty-one she told me to stop. I stopped with one foot still lifted, afraid that putting it down would be another step. She moved her hand to my shoulder and turned me slightly. A handle pressed into my back. She reached past me and opened the door.

“Why did we do that?”

“Because the corridor length changes when you look at the end.”

“How did you notice that?”

“You stopped hearing your own footsteps echo.”

I hadn't noticed. Even after she told me, I couldn't remember when the echo had stopped. I'd been looking at the numbers on the doors.

There were three identical staircases beyond that door. She closed her eyes and listened before choosing the middle one.

“What did you hear?”

“Ventilation.”

“They all sound the same.”

“No.”

They did to me. I followed her because I couldn't think of a better way to choose.

At the next landing she opened a door and slammed it shut so quickly I didn't see inside. Something struck the other side hard enough to bend the metal toward us. She still had her hand on the handle. I saw her pull it away and take a step back.

“Wrong?” I asked.

“Very.”

“What was there?”

“Don't know.”

“You looked.”

“Not long enough.”

We left it shut. I stayed close behind her, but I couldn't stop looking back at the door. Until then I'd thought she might recognize everything as soon as she saw it. Now I knew she could open the wrong thing just as I could.

Eventually we reached a landing where the next flight stopped in darkness. I mean the steps themselves stopped. I could see concrete, a broken end, and then no floor or wall beyond it. I held the rail and tried to lean far enough to see where the rest of the staircase went.

She crouched and threw another coin. We waited. There was no sound of it landing.

A scream came from below us. It sounded closer than the one behind the door.

“What now?” I asked.

She looked into the gap, then down the way we'd come. Her right hand opened and closed. When she moved her coat aside, I saw two black leather gloves draped over her belt. They'd been tucked so close to her waist that I hadn't noticed them before.

She took them from the belt and pulled one on, then the other. After checking the fit at her fingers and wrists, she rolled up both sleeves, folding the coat cuffs and the blouse beneath clear of her hands. She checked the folds again before holding one hand away from her clothes.

A blue point appeared against the leather of her forefinger. She closed her fist and it vanished.

“What was that?”

“Nothing.”

“That was not nothing.”

“Forget it.”

The scream came again. She looked down at her gloved hands, then at me.

“Your jacket. Off.”

I looked down. I was still wearing it, though I'd been pulling the sleeve up to read my arm for what felt like hours.

“Over your head,” she said. “Tuck your hands in. All the skin you can cover.”

“Why?”

She held up a gloved hand. “Because you haven't got these.”

My zip caught halfway down. I pulled until the little tab bent, then worked one arm free and dragged the other sleeve over my watch. She watched the stairs while I struggled with it.

“Will that burn me?”

“If you're exposed.”

“What about you?”

She pressed the seam at her wrist. “That's what the gloves are for. Hurry.”

She looked into the gap once more and said, “No.” I didn't know whether she was talking to me. Before I could ask, something began climbing the wall below us.

Its head came into view sideways. Then I saw the shoulders, and arms unfolding around the concrete. There were six. I counted because I couldn't understand how it was holding on. Each time one hand let go, another reached above it.

The woman drew the pistol again and fired. The first shot tore through its face. It kept climbing. She fired twice more, and it came faster.

She pushed me behind her. “Stay there.”

I backed toward the wall with the jacket bunched in my hands. The gun was very loud in the stairwell. After several shots it stopped, and I saw that the top part was stuck back. She dropped the magazine. It hit a step and slid while her other hand came back with a spare. She had that one in before the first had finished rattling.

For the next three shots I thought she was going to stop it. A shoulder jerked, and one hand came off the wall. I could see the bullets doing something. But another hand took the weight, and the damaged face kept coming up between them.

I tried to pull the jacket over my head. A grey finger hooked around the rail beside me and caught one of the sleeves. The cloth pulled tight across my throat. I couldn't get it off, and for a moment I couldn't get enough air to tell her.

She fired past my shoulder. The finger opened. My jacket dropped with it, sliding between the steps before I could catch the collar. I reached after it and stopped myself against the rail.

When I turned back, I had my hands over my ears. My mouth was open, but I couldn't hear whether I was shouting. She was still firing. Then the pistol stopped again, and the thing put a hand on our landing.

She looked at the gun, then at the hand.

“Oh, have it.”

She threw the pistol into its face. I heard the solid knock when it struck. The head went sideways and two of the arms flung out to catch its weight. She was already pulling off her coat.

The lining came over my head before I understood what she meant to do. She pushed me down beside the wall and drew the coat over my knees.

“Hands inside. Don't move.”

“I can't see.”

“Good.”

I caught the lining in my teeth when I tried to answer. It was warm from her body, and I could smell a faint trace of perfume. My right wrist was still outside. I pulled it in, trying to gather the hem under it.

The hem followed my hand. Just a little, enough to cover the strip of skin I'd left exposed. I thought she'd tucked it there, but I could hear her boots moving away from me. I tried to remember where her hands had been before she stood up.

There was still a small opening beside my cheek. Through it I saw grey concrete turn blue so suddenly that I could make out every pit in the wall.

Her gloved hand struck it. Fire ran down to the yellow stripe, followed the paint and stopped at the broken end of the landing. I thought it would spill over the edge. Instead, it turned upward.

I watched it go past where the ceiling ought to have stopped it. Another yellow edge lit above us, and another above that. Through the gap I could see landings hanging at angles they couldn't have held in the building. Each had the same chip in the paint beside her hand. Some looked close enough to touch, though I knew how many steps we'd climbed between landings.

I couldn't feel heat against my face. Outside the coat, the rail made a thin sound, as if it were being pulled tight.

The thing reached into the nearest line of fire. I saw its wrist below us and its fingers in the flame overhead. I looked from one to the other, trying to follow the arm between them. For a moment both stayed where they were. Then the fingers opened and fell upward out of sight.

I tried to crawl farther under the coat. The opening beside my cheek drew shut. I didn't feel a hand doing it. The cloth settled against my face, slowly and firmly, while the woman made a strained sound several feet away.

“Stay down.”

I stayed where she'd put me. I couldn't see her anymore, but I could hear her, and I needed her to keep talking.

Something hit the wall beside my covered head. I felt the impact through my teeth. Dust fell into the lining. I held both hands against my chest and counted my fingers, which sounds foolish now, but they were the only things I could check. I got to four and had to straighten my thumb from under my palm before I could make myself count it.

The creature shrieked. Claws scraped from the stairs onto the wall beside me. I put both hands over my head, with the coat still between them and my hair. The blows went upward, stopped, then came down again in a hurry. Blue light showed through the stitching.

I waited for the scraping to come back. Instead, the coat lifted from my face and she was crouching in front of me. Both gloves were still on. Blood ran from one nostril to her lip. She wiped it with the back of her wrist and looked at the smear.

The blue edges overhead flickered. She put a hand against the wall until they steadied, then caught my arm.

“Move.”

“But there's nowhere.”

“There will be.”

There was an emergency door beside us. She kicked it twice, but it didn't open. I stood with the coat around my shoulders while she examined the frame and the wall, then looked back at the place where the staircase ended.

“It's copying transitions,” she said.

“What?”

“Every time we choose a route it gives us another route.”

The scream came from behind us. She looked at the closed door for another moment.

“So we stop choosing.”

She turned her back to it and waited. I stood beside her because I didn't know where else to stand. Her coat reached the floor in a heap around my feet. I kept my fingers away from the hem.

“What are you doing?”

“Nothing.”

“That thing is coming.”

“I know.”

“What are we waiting for?”

“For the floor to decide.”

I thought she'd run out of things to try. I didn't say it because I was afraid she'd agree with me.

Something scraped along the stripe. I began to turn toward it.

“Here,” she said.

I looked at her instead. Blood had reached the corner of her mouth. Her hand shook against the wall, and I could hear her trying to slow her breathing. She wasn't looking at the creature either.

The door handle pressed into my back. All I wanted to do was turn around and try it. There might have been a corridor on the other side. Even another wrong corridor would put a door between us and that thing.

“How long?” I asked.

She didn't answer. Then the handle moved down against my back. I reached for it without thinking, and she caught my wrist. We stayed where we were.

An elevator chimed behind us.

I turned enough to see open doors where the emergency door had been. She gave a small smile.

“There.”

The creature struck the blue boundary. The fire flickered and she winced.

“Now.”

We ran toward the elevator. Its display showed **∞**. I saw that instead of a floor number and stopped at the entrance. After all the other signs, I couldn't make myself put a foot across.

She dragged me inside. As the doors closed, a blue-burning hand reached into the gap. She kicked it away, and the doors met. I pressed my back against the wall and watched the ceiling while the elevator began to move.

She pushed the coat down from my head but left it around my shoulders. Her hand rested on the seam until it stopped shaking so badly. Then she peeled one glove back and checked underneath.

Unbroken skin. No blister. She looked around the cuff before pulling it back into place.

Her nose was still bleeding. She pressed a folded tissue against it and leaned forward. There was blood on her lip too.

“You could do that the whole time?” I asked.

“No.”

“You just did.”

“I could also jump out a window.”

I looked at her. She kept the tissue against her nose.

“That doesn't make it useful.”

When the doors opened, I could see my office again. The same fluorescent lights and carpet. Floor Six. I wanted to go out immediately, but she stayed in the elevator, looking at the ceiling, the floor, the evacuation plan and the fire extinguisher.

She threw another coin. It landed on the carpet and stayed there. She stepped out, and I followed.

The stairwell clock said **22:55**. Six minutes after I'd gone into the elevator. I started laughing again, though I wasn't finding anything funny. I couldn't make six minutes fit with how tired I was.

She sat down hard against the wall. She was breathing heavily, and when she took off her gloves she had to pull them free one finger at a time. She draped them over her belt and tried to fasten them there. The fastening kept slipping out of her fingers.

I tried to give her the coat. She shook her head.

“Keep it on a minute.”

“You're hurt.”

“Yes.”

“Because of me?”

She looked up. “Don't flatter yourself.”

That nearly made me laugh properly. I sat with the coat held shut while she took out her phone. Hers had signal. She called someone and said, “I'm finished here. Your turn.”

She listened for a moment.

“Yes. Both of us.”

She sounded annoyed at having to confirm it. I watched the tissue redden in her hand.

“No, no medical.” She looked me over, from my face to my shoes. “He seems all right.”

I looked at her.

“Relatively,” she added.

After listening again, she covered the phone and asked whether I was religious. I thought I'd misheard her.

“What?”

“Religious.”

“Why?”

“Just answer.”

“No.”

“Family?”

“My mother went to church.”

“What kind?”

“Catholic.”

She uncovered the phone. “Catholic, apparently. Though he doesn't seem particular.”

There was another pause.

“I was going to suggest the Orthodox. The food's better.”

She held the phone away from her ear, then brought it back. “Well, you asked.”

By the time she ended the call, I'd moved a little farther away from her.

“Whose turn?” I asked.

“What?”

“You said it was somebody else's turn. Who did you call?”

She folded the tissue to find a clean part. “People who are going to clean this up.”

“Clean what up?”

She gestured around us. “This.”

“There's nothing here.”

“Exactly.”

I looked along the corridor. She stayed on the floor and didn't reach for me. I wasn't sure whether that meant I could leave or whether she knew I couldn't get far.

“What are you?”

She sighed. “That's becoming a complicated question.”

“Police?”

“No.”

“Military?”

“No.”

“Government?”

“Sometimes adjacent.”

I waited for her to explain that, but she only rubbed her forehead.

“Why did you ask about my mother?”

“The church. I was asking about the church.”

She stood carefully, putting her used tissue into her other hand.

“I'm afraid you're going to have to surrender yourself to the Church for a few weeks.”

“What?”

“Temporarily.”

“The Church.”

“Yes.”

“Which church?”

“Technically, yours will be easiest.”

“I just told you I'm not religious.”

“They won't mind.”

I laughed once. “You're kidnapping me.”

She winced. “That is an unnecessarily hostile description.”

“What would you call it?”

“A stay with people who know what to do.”

“That sounds worse.”

“Yes.”

“Are you insane?”

“Possibly, but not about this.”

I started walking away. I hadn't decided where I was going, except away from the conversation. At the end of the corridor the elevator was standing open. I stopped as soon as I saw inside it.

I looked toward the stairs. I knew what the stairs had done too. For a while I stood between the two, trying to choose the one I could make myself use. Then I looked back at her.

She raised one eyebrow. “That's approximately the problem.”

I stayed.

“What happens at the Church?”

“You sleep.”

“What else?”

“You eat.” She considered it. “Three meals a day.”

“That is your sales pitch?”

“The beds are decent.”

“You're joking.”

“No.”

I let out another laugh. She waited for me to finish.

“They'll ask questions. A lot of questions.”

“And if I refuse?”

Her expression changed. I couldn't work out whether she felt sorry for me or was simply too tired to keep arguing.

“I strongly recommend that you don't make this complicated.”

“Will they hurt me?”

“I don't expect them to.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No.”

“What will they do to my head?”

She went quiet. I hadn't known what I meant by the question until she failed to answer it.

“What will they do?”

“You'll have to ask them.”

“That sounds like you'll brainwash me.”

She looked away. “That's a vulgar description.”

“Is it wrong?”

I waited. Eventually she said, “I can't answer for them.”

I backed away again. “No.”

“I understand.”

“No, you don't.”

“I probably understand better than you think.”

“You rescued me just to hand me over.”

“Yes.” Before I could answer, she continued. “I can't leave you outside your office pretending you're going to finish the evening normally.”

“And they can?”

“They can stay.”

“I don't belong to the Church.”

“That has never mattered as much to the Church as people imagine.”

I didn't find that reassuring. She must have seen something of that in my face, because she tried again.

“They are not going to recruit you.”

“I should be grateful?”

“They are not going to make you clergy.”

“Wonderful.”

“They are not going to kill you.”

I stopped moving. She closed her mouth as if she'd like to take that one back.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You just said they're not going to kill me.”

“I was listing positives.”

“That was on the list?”

She closed her eyes. “Forget I said that.”

“I cannot forget anything about tonight.”

She opened them again. “That is precisely the issue.”

Three people came along the corridor: two men and a woman, all in dark civilian clothes. One man wore a clerical collar. He kept looking at the doors as he approached, which wasn't how I expected a priest to arrive. The other man checked the walls, the ceiling and the cameras. The woman carried a small first-aid case.

“Lynleit,” the man in the collar said.

She looked up. I repeated the name in my head. Until then I'd had nothing to call her if she walked away.

He glanced at the tissue and the gloves on her belt. “Bad?”

“It stopped.”

He looked toward me, and she gave a slight shake of her head. He didn't ask another question then.

The woman with the case approached me. I stepped back. Lynleit moved between us and said, “Slowly.” The woman stopped. I looked from one to the other, waiting to see whether they would argue, but she stayed where Lynleit had stopped her.

“Can I see your hands?” she asked.

I held them out. She turned them over, checked a scrape beside my thumb and asked me to follow her finger with my eyes. Then she asked my name and where I worked. I could answer those. It helped to have something I could answer.

“What time do you usually wake up?”

“Half past nine.”

The man in the collar nodded. “Unfortunate. Mass is at eight.”

I looked at Lynleit. She was still trying to fasten her gloves to her belt, and she didn't look up.

“We've spoken to your relatives,” the woman said. “They know you're being looked after. You won't have to deal with their calls tonight.”

“My sister?”

“Yes.”

There were missed calls on my phone now. I couldn't remember giving any of them her number.

“I want to talk to her.”

“When we're downstairs.”

The other man had opened the stairwell door. He held it and looked down the steps while we waited.

“Lynleit.”

She turned to me. It felt odd saying her name after following her for so long without knowing it.

“Will I remember you?”

She looked toward the Church people before answering. “I don't know.”

I looked at the tissue in her hand. There was blood on it, and I was still wearing her coat. I wanted to be certain I'd remember at least those things.

“Will I remember Floor Forty-Seven?”

“Probably.”

“Will I believe myself?”

She paused again. “Probably less.”

I was frightened by how much I wanted that. I didn't want to lose what had happened, but I wanted to be able to stand beside an open elevator without feeling the ceiling come down.

The woman offered me a hand. I got up without taking it. Then I remembered the coat and lifted it from my shoulders carefully. It didn't resist or move by itself. Lynleit put it on, and the hem brushed her boots. I couldn't see the gloves anymore.

She gave me a tired nod. “That's better.”

I took three steps toward the others, then turned back.

“Why did you come for me?”

She looked puzzled. “You were trapped.”

“That's it?”

“Yes.”

“You're still bleeding.”

She checked the tissue. “Not now.”

There was a small split where blood had dried on her lip. I pointed to it.

“And that?”

“A few days. It'll heal.”

“You could have died.”

She shrugged. “Possibly.”

I kept looking at her. I wanted her to say something that made the risk reasonable, but she only frowned.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“Then go.”

As I walked toward the stairwell, I heard the man in the collar ask what she'd established.

“Don't use the numbers,” she said.

“What else?”

“Keep a hand on the rail. Don't trust where it says you're going.”

“Anything else?”

“I'll write it down.”

“You left something in there?”

There was a pause.

“My pistol.”

The stairwell door closed before I heard his reply. They'd suggested using the elevator, but I refused. We took the stairs, and I counted every landing on the way down. There were six.

For the next seventeen days I lived in a monastery that officially didn't admit having me. The beds were good, which irritated me more than it should have. There were three meals a day too. She hadn't lied about either of those things.

The rest was harder to describe. Some of the priests didn't speak the way I expected priests to speak. They wanted me to repeat parts of the account, particularly Floor Forty-Seven, until I couldn't tell whether I was describing a place or giving the wrong answer to a question about stress.

An ordinary doctor examined the scrape on my hand and asked about my sleep. When I began telling him about the doors, the woman who'd brought me interrupted to explain that I'd had an accident at work. The questions about doors resumed after he'd left.

Nobody simply told me magic wasn't real. They gave me other ways to describe what I remembered. By the time I left, I could explain the clocks and the corridors in terms that sounded reasonable. I could also remember putting my hand against that window and finding that the view wouldn't move.

I still don't know how to put those two things together. Sometimes I find myself starting with one of their explanations before I've even decided what I want to say.

I went back to work eventually. I used the stairs. I still do.

Months later, I saw a woman in a long blue coat on the opposite railway platform. She had dark hair and stood looking at her phone. Something about the way she held herself made me keep looking.

She looked up, and for a second I thought I knew her. I was trying to remember her name when a train passed between us. After it had gone, I couldn't find her on the platform.

I stood there with my own phone in my hand. I couldn't remember why I'd taken it out. I kept looking across, trying to place her, and all I could bring to mind was a number.

Forty-seven.
