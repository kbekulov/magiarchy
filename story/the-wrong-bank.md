# The Wrong Bank

*The River Choir, Part III. Kyrien.*

[Part II: The Water Beneath Her Feet](../story.html?chapter=the-water-beneath-her-feet)

## Behind the trees

My knee went into the mud, slipped, and stayed there on the second attempt. I pulled against the root until my chest was above it. After that I could drag the rest of myself out.

I lay on the bank with one foot still in the water. It took me a moment to notice. I brought it up quickly and caught the heel on the root. Nothing was holding me. I checked anyway, pressing a hand around my ankle through the soaked cloth.

Across the river, Felix's light was moving at the steps. I could see heads below it. Someone was coughing. Reiner was there, or a man large enough to be Reiner, helping another person over the edge. I counted three shapes and then four, but one of them might have been the same person moving.

I couldn't see Lynleit.

There was no sensible reason to remain flat where anything in the water could reach my legs. I crawled higher, got one foot against a tree and stood with my shoulder on the trunk. The ground felt unreliable after the river. When I looked down, it wasn't moving.

I went inside the tree line and crouched behind a thick trunk. From there I could see through the lower branches without standing against the open bank. The river nearest me was visible. Farther out, the mist hid the spaces between the boats. I tried to find the one we had been on. A dark hull turned, showed me wet boards and went out of sight.

I should have called. I knew that. But I hadn't identified what had taken us out of the boats, and I didn't want to announce where I had come out until I knew whether I needed to move again. I listened for her and watched the near water.

Something struck it from below.

I saw a gloved hand, dark hair, a face rising far enough to cough. She reached in front of her. I moved out from the trunk, looking for anything long enough to put between us.

Then her shoulder came higher.

I thought she had found the hull. I couldn't see it beneath her, but I had lost track of the boats. She brought a knee up, moved her foot and crouched.

There was no hull.

I leaned to one side to see past a branch. It changed the angle. I could see water beneath both boots, the small rings spreading from them, her hand planted ahead of her. There was no board joining the places where she rested her weight.

She stood.

I stayed half out of cover with my hand on the trunk. She was too far from the mud to be standing on a shelf of the bank. I had just been in that water. I knew where I had failed to find the bottom.

I looked for something else beneath her. I don't know what I expected to find. Anything with an edge would have helped.

The surface dipped under a boot when she moved it. Water ran away from the sole. She put her weight there and remained upright.

I went back behind the tree.

I didn't want her to turn and see me staring while I was still trying to decide whether I understood what I was looking at.

There was another person in front of her.

I had missed the arrival. The figure was pale, with long hair and a dress that reflected little pieces of light when the water moved. I couldn't see the eyes from where I was. I could see the angle of the head. It was tipped towards Lynleit.

Neither of them came towards the bank for several seconds. Lynleit held her arms slightly away from her sides. She didn't look comfortable. The other figure scarcely moved at all.

Lynleit said something I couldn't hear.

I watched the distance between them. If the pale one closed it, I would have to do something. There was a branch beside my foot, too short to reach the water. I picked it up before deciding what use it could be. Then Lynleit stepped backwards.

The other figure remained where it was.

She took another step. She was walking on the water. I had run out of less difficult ways to describe it to myself.

I could have called her name. Instead I watched until she reached the reeds. She caught a branch, bent beneath it and began climbing the mud. Only then did I put the piece of wood down.

She called me from below the trees.

“Here.”

I came out where she could see me. She stopped with one hand still on the branch.

“Can you stand?” I asked.

“I am standing.”

She tried to take the next step and had to use the branch again. I went down far enough to offer my forearm. She took it without another answer. Her grip was hard. I braced against the tree and let her do the climbing.

When she was beside me, she didn't let go immediately. She was looking at my face.

“The others?”

“City bank. I saw people at the steps.”

“How many?”

“I couldn't count.”

She released my arm and tried to look through the trees herself.

## A thoughtful gesture

Her jacket was gone. Her hair stuck against her neck and shoulders; water ran from the ends of it onto her blouse. One glove was still clenched around the branch after she had let go of me.

I took my jacket off. It was heavy with water. I looked at it, then at her, and lowered it before offering it.

“A thoughtful gesture,” she said.

“I reconsidered it.”

“Sensibly.”

I folded it over my forearm. My shirt was wet enough that the change made little difference except to the weight. She pulled off one glove with her teeth at the fingertips, then used the freed hand for the other. She was having trouble holding them together.

“Put them away first,” I said.

She looked at me. I pointed at her belt. She put them there, carefully, and rested her empty hand against the tree.

I could hear someone calling from the other bank. Lynleit started towards the gap in the branches. After two steps she stopped and coughed so hard that she bent at the waist.

I waited beside her. When I touched her shoulder she lifted a hand, asking me to wait too.

“I need a moment,” she said.

There was a broad tree a little higher up the bank. Its roots made a place to sit without sliding towards the river. We got there slowly. I went behind her because somebody ought to keep looking at the path, and because she had already taken the better place against the root.

We ended up back to back. At first only our shoulders touched. She shifted, I moved my foot out of the way, and we found a position where neither of us had to hold the other upright. There wasn't much warmth in it, but there was less wind against the wet cloth.

I kept my jacket across my knees. She was still coughing. When she stopped, I could feel how quickly she was breathing through the contact at my back.

“Any pain?” I asked.

“Several varieties.”

“Something that stops you walking.”

“No.”

She asked me the same thing. I told her my ankle hurt but worked. She wanted to see it. I said we could do that when we were somewhere she didn't have to get up to look. She accepted that more readily than I expected.

Across the river the light moved along the wall. There were gaps between the trees where I could follow it. When it vanished behind one trunk, I watched the next gap instead of standing to recover the whole view.

I had seen something I could not fit into what I knew. I kept trying to put a support beneath her feet, even while remembering the water bending under them. If I had been told this by somebody else, I would have asked where he stood and what obstructed his view. I had already tried those questions on myself.

Lynleit shifted against me.

“You came ashore on this side.”

“It was closer.”

“So you could see the river.”

“Parts of it.”

She waited. I had stopped breathing without noticing.

“Did you see anything strange?”

“The boats had no crews. Something tried to drown all of us. We are sitting inside a quarantined park in wet clothes.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“It is what happened.”

She turned slightly. I felt the movement before I saw the edge of her face.

“And nothing else?”

“Nothing I can explain.”

It wasn't an answer to the question she wanted to ask. I knew that. She hadn't asked it either.

Her head rested back against my shoulder.

“Then do not invent an explanation.”

“I was not planning to.”

She was afraid I had seen her. That was what I made of the questions, the pause, the way she had looked at me when I came out of the trees. I didn't know why being seen was dangerous to her. For the moment I could leave her without a clear answer. I had spent enough time being invited to complete somebody else's account of an event.

The light on the opposite bank stopped and swept towards us. I kept still for another moment. Her breathing had begun to slow.

Then I said, “They're looking here.”

She raised her head at once.

## The way back

We moved to the gap where we could be seen without going down to the water. I called Felix's name. He turned the light, found us and kept it there.

I raised a hand. Lynleit called that we were both ashore.

For a moment everybody tried to speak across the river. I could hear Felix and Reiner but couldn't separate the words. Lynleit stopped trying to compete with them and looked for her phone.

It had stayed in its closed pouch. Her hands were shaking enough that she couldn't open it neatly. I held the edge while she worked the fastening. The screen lit when she pressed it. I hadn't expected that small thing to be as reassuring as it was.

She called Felix and put the phone between us on speaker, low enough that we both had to lean towards it.

“Reiner?” she asked.

“Here.”

“Maren?”

“Here.” Felix turned away from the phone. I heard him ask her something, then her irritated answer. He came back. “And displeased.”

“Ivo?”

“Here,” Ivo said himself.

She closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them, she asked whether they could walk.

Maren could, but not without help yet. Reiner said he would stay with her until she was steady. There was no suggestion that anyone should get back into the river.

“It was hands,” Maren said somewhere behind the phone.

Felix asked what she meant.

“On my legs. Someone was holding my legs.”

I looked down at my ankle. Lynleit was looking at the screen. Neither of us spoke into the little silence that followed.

Reiner asked where we were in relation to the bridge. I could see the side of it through two gaps upstream. Lynleit said we would take the path back to the barrier.

“Stay together,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Not the bank. The path.”

“Reiner.”

“The bank drops away before the bridge.”

She looked upstream along the mud and said, more quietly, “Understood.”

Felix would meet us at the locked end. He had to restore the cameras before leaving the van again. For a few moments his part of the conversation was addressed to his terminal instead of us. I heard him swear, stop, and say he had it.

Lynleit asked how long the walk around to the bridge would take him.

“Longer if you keep asking.”

“Then stop answering.”

He told us to call when we could see the barrier. She ended the call and stood looking at the river with the phone still in her hand.

“What?” I asked.

She shook her head and put it away.

I wanted to ask what she had seen from the water. I wanted to know whether the pale figure was a person, whether it had gone after the others, and whether it could come onto the bank. Every version of the first question gave away that I had been watching. I hadn't decided whether avoiding it was worth not knowing the answer.

“Do we need to keep watching the river?” I asked instead.

“For now.”

That was enough to keep me from treating the path as safety.

## Along the path

The path was a little higher than the place where we had rested. We climbed between roots and found paving under wet leaves. Rain had begun while we were sitting. I hadn't distinguished it from the water dripping out of the trees until it struck my face beyond them.

Lynleit went first. I wrung what I could out of my jacket and carried it over my arm. It wasn't going to dry there. My sleeves were sticking at the elbows, so I pushed them up and kept the knife where I could reach it. I had no particular idea what a knife would do to anything we had encountered. I wanted to know where it was.

There was a bench beside the path. Water stood on its slats. Farther ahead, around a bend we couldn't see from the bridge, a single amber lamp showed through the mist. It lit the pavement beneath it, not the trees overhead.

We went towards it because the path went towards it. I watched the edge nearest the river. Lynleit kept looking back at me.

The third time she did it, she stopped walking.

“I think you saw something, and don't want to tell me.”

I looked past her. The lamp was reflected in several shallow puddles, broken by leaves. A dark length lay among the branches over the next stretch of path. I couldn't tell whether it joined either tree.

“Shouldn't we leave as soon as possible?”

She watched me for another moment, then turned and gathered her wet hair with both hands.

“Are you scared?”

“Yes.”

She glanced back again. I don't think that was the answer she had expected.

“So am I,” she said. “Keep walking.”

I did, though I moved to where I could see past her shoulder. She pulled the length of her hair free of her collar. Her hands kept returning to it even after it was clear.

“There was a girl,” she said.

I waited.

“In the river.”

“Did she touch you?”

“No.”

She looked towards the water. I could see it only in narrow pieces between the trunks. She described sewn eyelids and fish fastened into a dress. I had seen the pale figure, but not those things. Hearing them didn't help me decide what it was.

“Dead fish?”

“Yes.”

“Was she one of the people on the boats?”

“I don't know.”

We hadn't found the people who ought to have been there. I didn't know if that was even the right way to ask it.

“And what pulled us down?”

She stopped winding her hair around her hand.

“I saw hands.”

“Maren said that.”

“I know.”

She didn't try to improve the answer. I looked at her hand tightening around the wet hair and decided not to ask her to describe them yet.

The dark length above the path moved.

I stopped.

At first I thought I had seen a branch change position because I was walking beneath it. I moved my head back to where it had been. The shape remained in its new position. Rain fell through the gap beside it.

Lynleit took another step before she noticed I wasn't with her.

“Kyrien?”

I lifted a hand. She waited.

There was something thin stretched between two larger branches. One part of it lay close against the upper branch. The other hung lower, then drew up a little as I watched. It had an end that might have been a head. I didn't want to decide that from the shape alone.

The branches didn't move with it.

I could see smaller twigs against the sky. They weren't being bent under weight or shaken by what was moving between them. That was the part I kept checking. If it was someone climbing, I should have seen something carry the weight.

“Don't go under that,” I said.

Lynleit followed my gaze.

For several seconds she stood with her back to me, looking up. I could see her fingers close and open once beside her shorts.

“Where did it start?”

I pointed to the upper branch without raising my arm all the way.

“There. Then lower.”

“I see it.”

I was glad she said that. I hadn't wanted another explanation about compression or a reflection.

She took a step back towards me, keeping her eyes up. The shape shifted once more. It didn't come down. It didn't speak. I heard rain, the river and a vehicle somewhere across it. I kept waiting for one of those sounds to stop so that I could hear something from above, but none did.

“The girl?” I asked.

“I don't know.”

This time she looked at me when she said it. I nodded. We left the question there.

## Past the bench

We couldn't stand and watch until we were too cold to move properly. I could already feel my fingers becoming slow. The path passed directly beneath the shape. To the river side, the paving dropped into roots and mud. On the other side there was enough open ground to go around the bench and rejoin the path beyond the overhanging branches.

Lynleit looked at both sides before I pointed.

“There,” she said.

“I can keep it in sight from behind the bench.”

She considered that, then moved with me rather than sending me around alone. We went slowly. I watched the ground when she watched above, and when I had found the next place to put my foot, I looked up again.

Wet leaves made it difficult to tell where the soil dipped. She caught my sleeve when I stepped onto an edge that crumbled. I moved back, found a root and went past it. Neither of us made anything of it.

The shape stayed among the branches. As our angle changed, one end disappeared behind the trunk. I waited until we could see past it. It was still there, or enough of it was there that I wasn't going to assume otherwise.

We reached the paving beyond the bench. Lynleit kept looking back until a bend took the branches out of sight.

“Did it follow?” she asked.

“Not while I could see it.”

We walked faster.

The amber lamp dropped behind us. Ahead, the path bent towards the bridge. I recognized the angle of the rail before I saw the barrier. There was a light beyond it and a person holding it at chest height.

Felix called Lynleit's name.

She answered. He lowered the light enough to see her face and said something under his breath that I couldn't hear.

The chain was still across the gate. Reiner was working at it from the bridge side. He had left Maren with Ivo on the city bank. He asked again whether both of us could walk. This time Lynleit said yes without sounding annoyed.

I turned towards the path while they worked. From the barrier I couldn't see the bench or the lamp. I couldn't see the shape. I listened for movement following ours and heard only water falling from the leaves.

The gate opened far enough for one person at a time.

Lynleit went through. I followed, and Reiner pulled it shut behind me. He put the chain back. The small metal noise was more welcome than anything Felix said.

Felix had a dry outer layer for Lynleit. She took it. I was glad he hadn't watched my earlier attempt.

He started to ask her something, looked at the way she was holding the fastening, and asked whether she could get to the van instead. She said she could. Reiner stayed close enough to catch either of us without making us hold him.

On the city side, Maren was sitting out of the wind with Ivo beside her. She looked at Lynleit, then at me, counting us as plainly as Reiner had. She raised a hand. I raised mine back.

I didn't tell anyone about the water.

Lynleit told Felix there had been movement above the park path. She described where, not what it was. He listened without making a joke and glanced at Reiner. Whatever they decided to do about it, it wasn't going to involve going back in that night.

Inside the van, the cards were still where we had left them.

I put my wet jacket on the floor instead of the shelf. Lynleit sat opposite me with Felix's dry layer around her shoulders. She looked at her unfinished hand, then at mine.

“Don't turn them over,” she said.

“I wasn't going to.”

She watched me long enough to make it clear that this was not a general expression of trust.

I slid her cards away from a drop falling off my sleeve. Then I put mine beside them and shut the box.
