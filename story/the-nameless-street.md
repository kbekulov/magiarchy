# Chapter 06: The Nameless Street

> Working draft based on the confirmed Nameless Street encounter. Exact dialogue, the route Lynleit and Felix take after lunch, and the sensory details of the street remain provisional.

## After Lunch

Felix had spent most of lunch explaining why a senior officer's secure telephone was not secure.

He continued after they left the restaurant, even though Lynleit had stopped answering him.

"The problem," he said, "is that people confuse being important with being difficult to overhear."

"You demonstrated this to him?"

"I demonstrated it to myself. Telling him would have made him unhappy."

"And not telling him?"

"Keeps both of us happy."

They walked without destination because neither had to return immediately. The older part of central Vilen rose and folded around them. Narrow streets bent between plastered facades and old brick. Timber gates opened into courtyards too deep to see from the road. Iron balconies hung above steep passages, and flights of stone steps connected streets built at different heights. A roof could appear level with the pavement on one side of a hill and three storeys below it on the other.

Lynleit liked this part of the city because it refused to be understood from a map. Felix liked it because no official driver could follow them without becoming angry.

He bought a folded newspaper from a street stall and carried it under one arm. Lynleit suspected he had no intention of reading it.

They turned past a small square where children chased pigeons around a dry fountain.

Felix said something about coffee.

Lynleit did not hear the end of the sentence.

## The Street Without a Name

She was alone.

The change had no edge. There was no doorway, sudden darkness, or moment in which Felix disappeared. One step belonged to their walk after lunch. The next belonged to a street she could not place.

Lynleit stopped.

Her head continued moving.

The walls leaned and corrected themselves. A pressure behind her eyes made distance difficult to judge. She placed one hand against cold plaster and waited for the dizziness to settle. It did not.

She had drunk no alcohol. Nobody at lunch had approached their table closely enough to drug her. She tested the thought twice because both conclusions felt remote, as though they belonged to someone reasoning in another room.

"Felix?"

Her voice travelled down the street and returned too softly.

No answer came.

There were no people at the windows. No voices behind the doors. No laundry moved above the courtyards. The street was not abandoned. Its doors were painted, its windows clean, and flowers stood in boxes beneath several sills. It appeared maintained by residents who had all agreed to leave a moment before she arrived.

Lynleit began walking.

The lane descended beneath an arch and opened onto a triangular courtyard. Three passages left it. She chose the widest one and emerged beside the same arch from the opposite direction.

She returned to the courtyard and took the passage with stone steps.

It climbed for longer than the hill could contain. At the top, the pavement sloped downward toward a row of doors whose numbers were not arranged in order. One bore a brass six. The next bore eighteen. The third had no number, only four empty holes where a plate had been removed.

Lynleit tried to remember whether the old city had a street like this.

Vilen had many streets whose names had changed with governments, dynasties, and wars. This one felt different. A name had not been removed from it. A name had never reached it.

The headache sharpened.

She heard a child crying.

## The Red Balloon

A little girl stood at the far end of the lane.

She wore a light coat buttoned to the throat. One hand held the string of a red balloon. Its color was the only clean color Lynleit had seen since losing Felix.

The girl looked at her.

For a moment neither moved.

Then the string slipped from the girl's hand.

The balloon rose slowly. Its thread dragged across the edge of a balcony, cleared the ironwork, and drifted down the street rather than into the open sky.

The girl cried out and ran after it. She turned through a narrow passage and vanished.

Lynleit reached for the thread as it passed. It caught across her fingers with almost no weight.

"Wait."

The girl did not return.

Her crying continued somewhere beyond the passage.

Lynleit could have released the balloon. She could have marked the courtyard and concentrated on finding Felix. She knew this before she followed.

The passage narrowed until her shoulders almost touched both walls. It opened at the top of another stairway, then divided between a covered arcade and an alley descending beneath houses. The crying came from the arcade.

She followed it.

At the next turn the sound came from below.

At the next, from behind her.

The girl never appeared. Lynleit saw only the red balloon above her hand, brushing stone whenever the street bent too sharply. The thread gave her something straight to hold while the city refused every other direction.

She crossed a small bridge between two buildings and found herself beneath it a minute later. She passed a blue door three times, although she never repeated a turn. A church tower appeared between the roofs, first ahead of her, then behind her, then close enough that she should have been able to touch its wall. No bell sounded from it.

The crying weakened into broken breaths.

"I have it," Lynleit called. "Stop running."

Her own voice sounded intoxicated. She could no longer keep one shoulder from striking the walls. The headache pulsed with her steps, and the pavement rose under her feet at angles that made no physical sense.

She followed because a child was lost.

The street did not need a stronger reason.

## The Broken Fence

The passage ended on a terrace above the lower city.

An old fence ran along its edge. Several sections had been repaired with newer timber. One had broken outward and left an opening above a sudden drop.

The girl stood there.

She was no longer crying.

She looked down through the broken fence with her hands resting at her sides.

Lynleit approached carefully. The red balloon pulled upward behind her.

"You should not stand there."

The girl did not turn.

Lynleit followed her gaze.

A boy lay below.

His body had come to rest against the base of a retaining wall. One arm was folded beneath him. His face was turned far enough away that Lynleit could not see it, but nothing in the angle of his neck belonged to a living child.

Her hand opened.

The red thread slid from her fingers. The balloon climbed above the terrace, passed the roofs, and became smaller against the colorless sky.

Lynleit looked at the girl.

The girl was smiling.

There was no grief in her face and no surprise that Lynleit had seen the body. She smiled as if the lost balloon, the crying, and the long pursuit had all arrived at the expected answer.

Lynleit tried to step back.

Her heel caught against the uneven pavement. The headache surged hard enough to empty the strength from her legs.

The girl placed both hands against her.

The push was not strong.

It did not need to be.

The broken fence passed beneath Lynleit's hands. Stone, roofs, and pale sky changed places. She saw the boy below growing larger. For a moment she understood that she would land beside him and that whoever found them would have no reason to believe one fall had anything to do with the other.

Then a white sheet struck her across the face.

## The Bench

"Lynleit."

The sheet struck her again.

"If you are dead, this is going to become extremely inconvenient."

She opened her eyes.

Felix stood over her, violently fanning her with the folded newspaper. Behind him, the ordinary city moved without concern. People crossed the square. A tram sounded somewhere beyond the buildings. The dry fountain was close enough for Lynleit to hear children running around it.

She was lying across a bench.

Felix stopped fanning.

"There you are."

Lynleit sat up too quickly and gripped the edge of the bench while the square tilted.

"How long?"

"Long enough for three strangers to decide I had murdered you and one to offer advice on reviving you."

"Where did you find me?"

Felix looked at the bench.

"Here."

"I mean before."

His expression changed. The playfulness withdrew without leaving his face entirely.

"You were beside me. Then you stopped answering. I thought you were ignoring me with unusual commitment." He crouched in front of her. "You took two steps toward the bench and collapsed."

Lynleit looked past him.

The old streets climbed behind the square exactly as they had before lunch. She could identify every entrance. None led toward the terrace, the blue door, or the silent church tower.

No red balloon crossed the sky.

Felix asked what she had seen.

Lynleit pressed two fingers against the place behind her eyes where the pain was fading.

"A street," she said.

Felix waited.

"Which street?"

She looked at the folded newspaper in his hand, then at the city around them.

"It did not have a name."

The answer sounded less credible once spoken aloud.

Felix did not laugh.

Several days later, when Lynleit looked down the hill at her father's body, the dead boy returned to her before she could stop him. For one terrible second the broken fence, the smiling girl, and Fionn occupied the same memory.

Helena needed no invented terror in Lynleit's face. She needed only to tell everyone what that terror meant.
