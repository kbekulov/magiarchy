## Doom Has an Address

### The condition

The Holumn was eventually destroyed.

That was the first mistake.

Lynleit and Kyrien did not understand what they were fighting until after they had already killed it. Destroying the creature completed the final part of its mechanism: its own terminal condition passed to whoever had caused its death.

They had destroyed it together. There was still only one curse, attached to both of them.

Their bodies began to weaken. Sleep stopped restoring them properly, and minor injuries healed too slowly. Lynleit found her magecraft increasingly exhausting. Kyrien began stopping on stairs. Within days, ordinary exertion had become difficult for both of them.

The Magi who examined the curse gave them several weeks.

Attempts to suppress it produced a second discovery. Removing it from Lynleit brought relief only until the attachment to Kyrien restored it. Treating him produced the same result in reverse.

One of them could survive if the other died first.

Lynleit rejected the answer.

She spoke to other Magi, searched Magiarchal records, and investigated prohibited theurgical procedures. Could memory erasure work? A coma? Destroying magical capacity? Every answer sent her back to the same choice.

Having one of them declared dead would not help either. The curse had no interest in legal language.

Eventually she called Natalia.

### The academically respectable answer

Natalia was silent long enough that Lynleit checked the connection.

"Natalia?"

"I'm here. Find something to write on."

Lynleit pulled a sheet out from under a report. "Should I put my name at the top?"

"Twice, if it helps."

"It rarely helped with your marking."

"Two names, then. Yours and Kyrien's."

Lynleit wrote them side by side.

"Join them," Natalia said. "Now cross yours out."

The pen stopped.

"On the paper, Lynleit."

"I know. I was enjoying the lesson."

"If I gave that sheet to someone who knew Kyrien, could they still find you?"

"Through him."

"And if you crossed out his name instead?"

"They could ask me."

"There. Suppression rubs out part of the address. It leaves someone who can supply the missing part."

Lynleit looked at the line between the names. She had drawn it through an old calculation.

"So they keep treating us as separate patients."

"Which is a sensible place to begin. Unfortunately, you have already tested it."

"Several times."

"And nearly exhausted yourselves proving the same thing. Keep that sheet. We're going to stop repeating it."

Lynleit drew a small, unfriendly face beside her name.

"This is older than your Holumn," Natalia continued. "The mechanism, I mean. People have been trying to move a death from one person to another for a very long time."

"With results?"

"The Assyrians had a substitute-king ritual. A fatal omen threatened the king, so another man occupied his place and received his fate."

"Comforting."

"The useful question is why the omen would accept the substitute. It wasn't looking for the king's face. It recognized a position someone else could occupy."

"And ours recognizes the people who killed it."

"Yes. Usually you hear stories about a creature hiding its life in an egg, or an animal, or a locked box. Killing the body doesn't finish the job."

Lynleit turned the pen over. "This one hid its death in us."

"Exactly."

She stopped drawing.

"Then the usual solution really is what they told me."

"One dies first."

The words settled badly between them.

Lynleit stared out the window. "I'm not doing that."

"I assumed as much."

"There has to be another way."

"There usually is. People just become lazy when death works reliably."

Lynleit closed her eyes. "Natalia."

"I'm serious."

"Then explain. I'll even stop decorating your diagram."

"What exactly do you think the curse recognizes?"

"Us."

"No. Think."

Lynleit exhaled through her nose. Natalia had used that tone in lectures too.

"The people who killed it," she said.

"And what makes you the same person now?"

"I called you so you could answer questions, not sit me down for an examination."

"Unfortunately, you've always needed both."

"Only because you refuse to send the answers."

"The ego," Natalia said. "Jung describes it as 'a complex of ideas which constitutes the centre of my field of consciousness.' Put that down. Leave space underneath."

"For the translation?"

"For your objections. The ego isn't the whole mind. It's the part around which you arrange what you consciously call yours. Your decisions, your memories, your intentions. You can forget what you ate yesterday without ceasing to recognize yourself."

"I haven't forgotten. I object to calling it food."

"Then the memory is doing very well."

Lynleit wrote the definition.

"So the curse doesn't need every memory intact."

"That would be my first objection to erasing them. You might lose a year and still make the same choices for the same reasons. We need to know what it would take for its old description of you to stop being enough."

"Can you measure that?"

"I once thought I could. I kept a notebook of my decisions for three weeks. Every choice, followed by the reason I thought I'd made it."

"How many notebooks?"

"Too many. I was very pleased with them."

"Always a warning."

"Eventually I noticed that the woman in the notebooks was wonderfully reasonable. She never ate because she was bored. Never postponed a letter because she disliked the recipient. She had excellent reasons for everything."

"You should introduce us."

"I couldn't. I'd made her up. By the time I wrote down a choice, I'd already tidied it."

Lynleit smiled and pushed the reports aside.

"What did you try instead?"

"I wrote down what I expected to do before I did it. Then what actually happened, including the little things I wanted to leave out. Tea, letters, unfinished work. The two columns disagreed much more often."

"And that worked?"

"It showed me where to look. It didn't explain every disagreement. Some days I was simply tired."

Lynleit divided the space below the quotation into two columns.

"Expectation and result."

"Yes. For you, that means a new description of yourself is not enough. Calling yourself fearless doesn't remove a fear. Losing a title doesn't necessarily change how you make a decision. Changing clothes certainly won't help."

"A pity. I could have been cured before lunch."

"You're still looking for a procedure you can complete without anything happening to you."

"I'm prepared to be mildly inconvenienced."

"Lynleit."

She put the pen down.

"How much has to change?"

"That I don't know."

"Helpful."

"I can give you a difference you already understand. When I left the Academy, I took my books home and thought I would begin all the work I'd been postponing."

"And did you?"

"I spent the first morning drafting a request to conduct it."

Lynleit laughed once, then pressed a hand against her ribs.

"To whom?"

"An excellent question. I had nowhere to send it."

"You could have sent it to me. I'd have approved it."

"You would have corrected my handwriting."

"Both. I'm generous."

"I had stopped being a professor on paper. I was still waiting for someone to permit the next experiment. That habit took longer to notice. Longer still to stop."

Lynleit picked up the pen again.

"So handing in your resignation wasn't the whole change."

"No. Nor would taking the title away from me now put me back where I was. I have done the work. Made mistakes I couldn't assign to the Academy. Paid for them myself."

"And you still give examinations over the phone."

"Some habits are worth keeping."

Lynleit looked at the two names.

"Trauma could do it."

"It can. So can a religious conversion, grief, parenthood. But none is a reliable instruction. Two people can survive the same event and make very different things of it."

"We already have a confrontation with death arranged."

"Yes. I was hoping for something less terminal."

"And both Kyrien and I would have to change."

"Most likely. Go back to your sheet. You crossed out one name and could still recover it through the other."

"So changing only me leaves him holding the old answer."

"That is the risk. Whatever happens has to change what each of you is to the other."

"Into what?"

Natalia went quiet again.

Lynleit knew that silence.

It was the silence Natalia used immediately before saying something she knew would irritate her.

"No."

"I haven't said anything."

"You were about to."

"I was."

"Natalia."

"Fall in love."

Lynleit stared at the phone.

For several seconds neither spoke.

Then Lynleit said, very carefully, "You cannot possibly be serious."

"I am entirely serious."

"You gave me homework, introduced me to Carl Jung, and told me about a dead substitute king so that you could tell me to fall in love?"

"When you phrase it like that, it does sound theatrical."

"I was trying to make it sound expensive. I don't intend to pay."

"Think about an ordinary evening. You're absorbed in something. Someone you care about was meant to call an hour ago. You read the same sentence three times. You decide to work for another ten minutes before checking your phone."

"And check it after two."

"Thirty seconds, in my case. Myka was quite safe. I'd spent an hour pretending I could concentrate while I waited to hear it."

"You could have called her."

"I did. She asked whether I needed help with something."

"Did you?"

"I needed her to stop sounding so pleased with herself."

Lynleit glanced at her own phone.

"All right. Someone matters to you. How does that become enough to save us?"

"It may not. Worrying over a missed call isn't a cure. But notice how much of an ordinary evening can already depend on someone else's safety. Now imagine finding you can't make plans for your own future without making room for that person. Even plans you never intend to show them."

"That is sentimental nonsense."

"No. Sentimental nonsense is what poets do with it afterward."

Lynleit said nothing.

"You don't have to become agreeable," Natalia added. "You may be thoroughly annoyed. The question is whether you still make the same decisions about your life once theirs has become part of it."

"And whether they do the same."

"Yes. That's why I'm talking about a reciprocal change. One person longing beautifully while the other carries on as before is no help to my hypothesis."

"You make it sound like surgery."

"At the moment, it is."

"And how exactly do you prescribe falling in love with someone?"

"You don't."

"Then this is useless."

"I didn't say it was convenient. You asked what might work."

Lynleit closed her eyes.

Natalia added, almost casually, "There are faster ways to produce similar changes, of course."

Lynleit opened them again.

"I dislike the way you said that."

"You should."

"What ways?"

"Ask someone else."

"Natalia."

"I've already given you the academically respectable answer."

"And the unrespectable one?"

Natalia laughed softly.

"Go speak to a priest."

"You're enjoying this far too much."

"I'm enjoying speaking to you. I'd prefer you weren't dying."

Lynleit's hand tightened around the pen.

"Then keep your afternoon free. I'm going to bring you a very unreasonable objection."

"I look forward to it."

After the call, Lynleit copied the two names onto a clean page. Underneath she wrote: **What would have to change in both?**

She folded Natalia's first diagram and kept it inside the notebook.

### The unrespectable answer

She sought a second opinion. Then a third. Eventually the investigation took her to Father Mikhail.

His study smelled of wax, old wood, and something medicinal. Lynleit could not locate the last smell. Possibly it was the priest.

Unlike Natalia, Mikhail had access to older Church records of curses attached to personal identity. He laid out several accounts while she took notes. Conversion had worked in some cases, marriage in others. War, parenthood, a confession followed by absolution: none had worked consistently.

"So it isn't the marriage," Lynleit said.

"Nor the certificate. The person afterward could no longer be described adequately as the person before."

"And what would you recommend for Kyrien and me?"

Mikhail folded his hands over his stomach.

Nearly a minute passed. Lynleit straightened in her chair.

"Do you understand your problem?" he asked.

"I take it you have a theory."

"I do."

"Then I'm listening."

He closed his eyes, rose, and went to the narrow window. In the evening light his cassock was almost black. When he spoke again, his voice had acquired the measured rise and fall of a chant.

"O great warrior of the Divine Whore."

Lynleit blinked. Her cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

"O heir to the blood that stood at Inanna's gates, made covenants beneath her sign, and returned from her altars with what others had to take by the sword."

She set the cup carefully on its saucer.

"Father Mikhail, I'm not sure that wording is appropriate."

"All the better. You're listening."

He clasped his hands behind his back.

"O daughter of a line that has carried its bond with the Cult through three thousand years. Through kingdoms, wars, prohibitions, reforms, and an inexhaustible supply of people convinced they had finally put an end to it."

Lynleit lowered her eyes to the tea.

"O heir to a tradition in which desire was power, intimacy could seal a bargain, the body could speak, and a bed could matter more to politics than a throne."

"Father Mikhail."

It was a very polite request.

He looked at her, waited, and continued.

"And here sits its representative. Educated. Disciplined. Dangerous. Entirely familiar with her ancestry."

Her fingers tightened on the armrest.

"And yet..."

He considered the wording. Lynleit closed her eyes.

"...catastrophically unsuited to the position."

"I would ask you not to develop that thought."

"Why?"

"I doubt it will benefit either of us. Or your priesthood."

"A reasonable objection."

Her shoulders eased.

"Overruled."

Mikhail returned to the table, sat, adjusted his sleeve, and drank his tea.

Then, in his ordinary voice:

"Just have sex, loser."

Lynleit stared at him. He took another sip.

She opened her mouth, closed it, and looked away. The heat had reached her ears.

"You are a priest."

"An observant one."

"I think we're finished."

"No. That was the introduction."

She glanced at the door.

"Retreat is hardly becoming of a warrior of Inanna, either."

"You're beginning to abuse your knowledge of my family."

"I'm using the pastoral material available."

"My genealogy is not pastoral material."

"Everything is pastoral material when the flock is sufficiently stubborn."

Lynleit clasped her hands in her lap.

"An association between two traditions does not automatically oblige anyone to reproduce every cultural feature of either."

Mikhail raised an eyebrow.

She stopped.

"How interesting."

"Don't."

"I haven't said anything."

"Which is why I'm asking you not to."

He reached for his cup.

"You've prepared a defence."

"A principle."

"Of course."

"A general principle."

"And you understand the charge."

"I understand that you've decided to turn genealogy into harassment."

She picked up her cup again.

"You can discuss murder, a coup, magical blackmail, and mass casualties in a perfectly even voice," Mikhail said. "One other human activity makes you an expert on porcelain."

Lynleit looked at the saucer, then at him.

"Perhaps the porcelain is behaving with more dignity than certain people present."

He gave a quiet snort.

"Good. Then let's leave theology out of it. You approach intimacy as an operation to be attempted after reconnaissance, risk assessment, political guarantees, and written opinions from several independent experts."

"I came here about a curse."

"And I am answering. If you like a man, you investigate him. If you like him a great deal, you begin counterintelligence."

Her lips pressed together.

"And when it becomes personal enough that neither is helping, you declare the matter irrelevant."

Lynleit looked toward the window. Mikhail let her.

"Thank you for the diagnosis."

"An observation."

"It has been noted."

"In the voice of an official sending a document to an archive nobody will ever open."

She set the cup down. This time she left it there.

"You seem to have confused my embarrassment with a treatment."

"No. You asked whether something could change both of you enough for the curse to lose its hold. If an attachment already exists, physical intimacy might do that. What you trust him with, what you expect from him afterward, what he can no longer pretend you are to him. You would both have to change."

"And if the attachment isn't sufficient?"

"Then you will merely have had sex."

Lynleit picked up her pen.

"So you cannot promise anything."

"No. I can tell you why this is a possibility. I cannot make it safe by explaining it until you stop blushing."

She wrote something, shut the notebook, and stood.

"I think that is more than enough spiritual guidance for one visit."

"You're leaving again."

"I'm concluding the audience."

"This is my study."

Lynleit straightened her jacket.

"All the more reason."

She went to the door.

"Lynleit."

"What?"

"You do understand why this is so funny?"

Her hand stayed on the handle.

"Unfortunately."

"Then we have reached an understanding."

She opened the door and looked back. Her face was composed again.

"If you ever call me a warrior of the Divine Whore in front of anyone else, I will find a canonically permissible way to make your life considerably less pleasant."

Mikhail considered this.

"Better."

"What?"

"Some passion in that threat."

She shut the door hard enough to rattle the cups.

In the corridor, Lynleit opened her notebook. **SEX?** occupied most of the page.

She nearly tore it out.

### In the study

Mikhail waited until her footsteps had receded, then looked at the door.

"Three thousand years of continuity."

He took a sip.

"And the result. A virgin."

He sighed.

"A disgrace to the institution."

He went on drinking his tea.

### An alternative

She did not tell Kyrien.

He knew the prognosis, the failed attempts at suppression, and that she was still searching. He did not know what Natalia had told her. He did not know what Mikhail had told her.

Most importantly, he did not know that Lynleit had begun treating their relationship as a possible procedure.

At first she refused the idea. Then she investigated it. Then she attempted to disprove it.

The Church called it transformation. Natalia called it reorganization of the ego. An old Magiarchal text called it discontinuity of identity; a thaumaturgical paper, failure of sympathetic recognition. Every source she consulted gave her another reason to return to the page she had nearly torn out.

Meanwhile, their remaining weeks became days.

### Two days later

Natalia called two days later.

"So."

"No."

"I haven't asked anything."

"You were about to."

"I was."

Lynleit continued reading.

Finally Natalia said, "How do you feel about Kyrien now?"

"The same."

"Really."

"Yes."

"Nothing changed."

"Nothing relevant."

"Interesting distinction."

"There is no distinction."

"Of course."

Lynleit turned a page she had not read.

Natalia waited again.

"The curse is gone," Lynleit said.

"It is."

"Kyrien is alive."

"He is."

"I am alive."

"Also true."

"The intervention therefore accomplished its purpose."

Natalia made a thoughtful sound.

"It failed."

"It worked."

"By failing."

Lynleit closed the book.

"There will not be another attempt."

"I didn't suggest one."

"There is no reason for one."

"Correct."

"The matter is resolved."

"Completely."

Lynleit narrowed her eyes.

Natalia said nothing.

That silence was intolerable.

"There is absolutely no reason whatsoever for Kyrien and me to repeat anything that happened that evening."

"I agree."

"Good."

"Good."

Another silence.

Then Natalia asked:

"Are you disappointed?"

Lynleit hung up.

She opened the book again. The page was unfamiliar. So was the one before it.

There had been a reason for that evening. A reason she could write down, defend, and blame on someone else. Now the curse was gone.

She put the notebook under the book and tried to read.

### Kyrien's account

Kyrien understood considerably less.

He knew Lynleit had tried to sleep with him while behaving as though she were attempting an assassination. He knew she refused to explain why. He knew the curse had begun weakening the following morning.

Any mention of that evening made her hostile enough to weaponize household items.

There was a connection between these facts. He was missing something.

This irritated him.

Lynleit intended to keep it that way.

The incomplete attempt and what followed had changed both of them enough for the curse to lose its hold. It could no longer recognize the exact pair of identities that had destroyed the Holumn.

They survived.

As far as Lynleit was concerned, the boundary between them remained uncrossed.

Mostly.
